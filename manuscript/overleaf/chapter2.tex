\chapter{Literature Review}\label{ch:literature_review}

This chapter situates the dissertation at the intersection of systems engineering practice, model-based systems engineering, and large language models, with the posture that if large language models are to be used in systems engineering workflows, then their qualification must be grounded in evidence that matches the kinds of work products and risks that systems engineering actually carries.

It first establishes the systems engineering context in which model qualification matters, emphasizing the role of complexity, lifecycle coupling, and system-of-systems characteristics that amplify consequence when errors propagate across artifacts and stakeholders. 

Followed by the contrast of document-centric engineering with model-centric workflows, highlighting how the digital thread changes what it means to generate, inspect, and verify engineering work products. 

The chapter then reviews LLMs capabilities and limitations for failure modes: hallucination, weak grounding, inconsistent reasoning under small perturbations, and sensitivity to context and prompting. These properties motivate why benchmark score alone cannot be treated as a single interchangeable proxy for deployable competence. 

The second half of the chapter focuses on evaluation practice. It surveys the evolution of modern benchmarking, including known failure modes such as data leakage, contamination, and dataset lifecycle challenges. It then turns to evaluation modalities, comparing and contrasting multiple-choice and open-ended formats and how they measure different facets of capability and produce different kinds of evidence. 

Across these bodies of work, the reviewed literature demonstrates substantial activity in artificial intelligence for systems engineering and in large language model benchmarking more broadly. However, it does not report a benchmark or evaluation framework explicitly designed to support qualification-oriented assessment of LLMs for systems engineering tasks. Existing benchmarks are largely domain-general, and prior AI-for-SE studies typically evaluate task performance without establishing reusable, reproducible evidence suitable for qualification or comparison across models and time \cite{serc_ai4se_2024, SERC_AI4SE_SE4AI_Research_Application_Workshop_Report_2025}.

This literature review therefore establishes the conceptual requirements for a benchmark-informed qualification approach in systems engineering, and it prepares the methodological foundations for the benchmark design, experimental protocol, and analysis developed in that chapters that follow.

This section motivates why \ac{se} constitutes a domain-specific environment for evaluating \acp{llm}. Systems engineering outputs are artifact-based and lifecycle-coupled: work products must remain configuration-managed, traceable, and auditable across a digital thread. The goal is not to claim that transfer from general-purpose benchmarks is impossible, but to argue that general benchmark performance alone is insufficient qualification evidence for \ac{se} intended use \cite{walden_systems_engineering_handbook_2015, standard_ieee_15288_2023, deputyassistantsecretaryofdefense_DepartmentDefenseDigitalStrategy_2018}

We develop this argument in three steps. First, we characterize the artifact network that \ac{se} produces and the evaluation implications of cross-artifact coherence. Next, we describe how digital engineering and \ac{mbse} shift \ac{se} toward model-/repository-centric baselines with larger failure surfaces for automated assistance on the digital thread. Finally, we discuss how scale and system-of-systems complexity amplify assurance demands, making cross-artifact failures more consequential than isolated textual errors.


\subsection{The Nature of Systems Engineering Artifacts}
\label{subsec:ch2_se_artifacts}

Systems engineering is commonly defined as a disciplined, integrative approach for designing, realizing, and operating complex systems across the full lifecycle. This definition emphasizes both technical integration and governance across time: \ac{se} coordinates diverse specialties into a coherent system that satisfies stakeholder needs within technical, cost, schedule, and risk constraints
\cite{incose_GuideSystemsEngineering_2025, maier_ArtSystemsArchitecting_2002, walden_systems_engineering_handbook_2015}. \ac{se} is a socio-technical practice in which decisions must be explainable to stakeholders, defensible under review, and revisable under evolving constraints \cite{BuedeMiller_EngineeringDesignOfSystems_2016, grady_SystemSynthesisProduct_2010, Blanchard_Fabrycky_Systems_Engineering_and_Analysis_2011, blanchard_system_engineering_management_2008 , Langford_engineering_systems_integration_2012}.

\ac{se} produces interdependent lifecycle work products including requirements, architectures, interface definitions, verification artifacts, trade studies, risk registers, and decision rationale whose quality is evaluated not only on a per-artifact basis, but also through cross-artifact consistency and traceability. Each work product is expected to link upstream intent to downstream verification and to evolve under change control \cite{standard_ieee_15288_2023, walden_systems_engineering_handbook_2015}. In a digital-thread, these links are meant to support audit, impact analysis, and assurance arguments across the lifecycle \cite{deputyassistantsecretaryofdefense_DepartmentDefenseDigitalStrategy_2018, Ouzzif_Leveraging_AI_Manage_Technical_Debt_Test_Evaluation_2025, Helmerich_Transforming_Systems_Engineering_Agentic_AI_2025}.


When \acp{llm} are inserted into \ac{se} workflows, the qualification question becomes whether the model can support drafting, extraction, transformation, and consistency-checking tasks without breaking this artifact network. In other words, the primary evaluation target is often not a single answer, but whether the model preserves or improves traceability, avoids introducing inconsistencies across artifacts, and produces outputs that remain verifiable within the digital thread \cite{nist_ai_rmf_2023, iso_iec_25010_2011, Traveller_Agile_Engineering_Agentic_Co_Modelers_2025, Ouzzif_Leveraging_AI_Manage_Technical_Debt_Test_Evaluation_2025}.

\subsubsection{From Document-Centric to Model-Centric Baselines}
\label{subsec:ch2_doc_to_model}

Historically, systems engineering practice has been dominated by document-centric approaches in which the system definition is distributed across collections of narrative artifacts, including requirements specifications, interface control documents, design descriptions, and verification plans. These artifacts are typically maintained as human-readable files including but not limited to: PDFs, Word documents, spreadsheets, and slide decks often stored in loosely coupled repositories. In such environments, traceability, consistency checking, and configuration control are largely enforced through manual processes: engineers update multiple documents by hand, maintain trace matrices explicitly, and rely on review discipline to detect contradictions and omissions. As system complexity increases, this mode of operation makes cross-artifact coherence increasingly difficult to sustain, and ambiguity regarding the authoritative system baseline becomes more likely \cite{incose_GuideSystemsEngineering_2025}.

Digital engineering and Model-Based Systems Engineering (\ac{mbse}) emerged in response to these limitations. \ac{mbse} is commonly defined as a paradigm that uses formalized representations of systems—models—to support the performance of systems engineering tasks throughout the system lifecycle, in contrast to legacy approaches \cite{incose_GuideSystemsEngineering_2025}. The \ac{sebok} similarly defines \ac{mbse} as "a paradigm that uses formalized representations of systems, known as models, to support and facilitate the performance of systems engineering tasks throughout a system’s life cycle'' \cite{incose_GuideSystemsEngineering_2025}. The central distinction is not simply the use of modeling tools, but the treatment of system definition as structured, governed data rather than as loosely synchronized artifacts.

In model- and repository-centric baselines, system elements are instantiated within a shared repository and rendered across multiple views depending on the framework \cite{OMG_UAF_1.3_2025, noauthor_dodaf_2010, zachman_zachman_2003}, but typically include structure and behavior derived from a common underlying model. When attributes change, inter-related analyses and views can update as alternate projections of the same data, reducing fragmentation and the need for manual reconciliation. Reported benefits of this approach include improved traceability, automated consistency checking, and enhanced stakeholder communication, particularly when models function as a single, governed source of truth that can be queried and transformed across lifecycle activities \cite{hart_IntroductionModelBasedSystem_2015}. This shift re-frames the technical baseline from a collection of documents to an interconnected artifact network maintained under configuration control.

At the same time, the literature consistently cautions against viewing \ac{mbse} as a panacea, arguing that poorly implemented \ac{mbse} initiatives often mirror and amplify existing deficiencies in systems engineering practice rather than correcting them, effectively "bastardizing" document-based workflows within a modeling environment \cite{lerat_ThreeReasonsWhy_2010, madni_HandbookModelBasedSystems_2023}.

\subsubsection{Transition Drivers and the Hybrid Reality}
\label{subsec:ch2_transition_drivers}

The transition from document-centric to model-centric \ac{se} is driven by a combination of technical, organizational, and strategic pressures. Increasing system complexity—particularly software intensity, interconnectedness, and long lifecycle spans—has made manual synchronization across heterogeneous artifacts increasingly untenable. At the same time, budget constraints and compressed delivery schedules have heightened the need for approaches that reduce rework, improve change impact analysis, and support earlier detection of integration issues.

Institutional digital engineering initiatives reflect these pressures to operate under increasingly restrictive budgets and aggressive delivery timelines, noting that legacy approaches are too slow to keep pace with adversaries \cite{NAVWAR_DE_Strategy_2023}.

Similar motivations underlie digital twin efforts in aerospace and defense, which emphasize the integration of physics-based models, operational data, and historical records to create authoritative digital representations of systems across their lifecycles. digital twin concepts that integrate high-fidelity simulation with health management and fleet data to improve safety and reliability \cite{glaessgen_digital_2012}, while Air Force is conducting efforts to develop a digital thread and digital twin analytical framework that merges modeling and experimental data to support lifecycle decision-making \cite{kraft_AirForceDigital_2016}.

Despite these strategic drivers, most organizations operate in a hybrid state rather than a fully model-centric one. Portions of the engineering baseline may reside in structured repositories with models, requirements databases, code, and configuration items, while other portions remain document-centric, including specifications, contractual deliverables, and slide-based decision artifacts.

The adoption of \ac{mbse} is frequently impeded by various barriers to entry, including training requirements, organizational inertia, tool cost, and difficulties integrating with legacy systems. As a result, organizations often pursue incremental transition strategies rather than wholesale replacement of document-based workflows \cite{systems_engineering_guidebook_2021, vaneman_mbse_demystified_2025}. One contributing factor to this high barrier to entry is the large number of competing frameworks, languages, methods, and tools, which increases the complexity of adoption and proficiency development \cite{OMG_UAF_1.3_2025,noauthor_dodaf_2010, zachman_ZachmanFrameworkEnterprise_2003, OMG_UML_2_5_1_2017, LML_Specification_2_0_2025, OMG_UPDM_2_1_2016, OMG_KerML_1_0_2025, OMG_BPMN_2_0_2_2014, Dassault_Cameo_Systems_Modeler_2025, Eclipse_Capella_Project_Capella_2026, Ansys_System_Architecture_Modeler_SAM_2026, IBM_Engineering_Rhapsody_2026, Spec_Innovations_Innoslate_2026, OpenMBEE_OpenMBEE_2026, PTC_PTC_Modeler_2026, MathWorks_System_Composer_2026}.

This hybrid reality is further complicated by ongoing standards and tooling transitions. Guidance on transitioning from SysML v1 to SysML v2 highlights the planning, migration, and interoperability challenges organizations must manage as modeling languages and ecosystems evolve \cite{OUSDRE_SysMLV1SysML2_2024, friedenthal_MovingForwardSysMLV2_2024, zhang_MBSE20More_2025}.


\subsubsection{Tooling for the Digital Thread}
\label{subsec:ch2_tool_for_digital_thread}

As systems engineering baselines become more structured and repository-managed, the scope for tool augmentation expands beyond drafting isolated documents to operating directly on artifact networks. In digital-thread-enabled environments, tools may support querying, transforming, reconciling, and propagating changes across linked lifecycle artifacts rather than generating narrative content \cite{kumar_AIEnabledDigital_2023}. This shift reflects the underlying premise of the digital thread: value is created by maintaining coherence across the artifact network under change.

Recent work has explored the use of knowledge graphs as a foundation for digital thread architectures, enabling explicit representation of relationships among requirements, design elements, verification activities, and operational data. Agentic-AI-enabled digital thread architectures have been proposed that leverage knowledge graphs, retrieval-augmented generation, and graph-based reasoning to deliver trusted, explainable lifecycle traceability across requirements, design, test, manufacturing, and operations \cite{kumar_AIEnabledDigital_2023, Jaskie_Agentic_AI_Lifecycle_Traceability_Digital_Thread_2025, Lipizzi_LLM_Augmented_Pipelines_Validated_System_Model_Creation_2025, Helmerich_Transforming_Systems_Engineering_Agentic_AI_2025}. 

Modern systems engineering practice will require the continuous maintenance of a governed artifact network supported by increasingly capable tools \cite{Adeyeye_AI_for_Systems_Engineering_2024}. Understanding this operational context is essential for examining how language models, when introduced into such environments, interact with systems engineering artifacts and where their contributions may succeed or fail. Although recent surveys of U.S. systems engineering curricula suggest that AI coverage is increasingly common, AI is often taught silo-ed rather than being embedded into core, lifecycle workflows \cite{Zhang_AI_Systems_Engineering_Education_Curricula_Training_US_2025}. The following section therefore focuses on the interaction of language models with systems engineering artifacts in digital-thread-enabled workflows.

\subsection{AI Foundations}

\subsubsection{The Field of AI and Where Generative AI Sits}

\acp{llm} fit into the AI landscape as a specific modern approach within a broader stack: \ac{ai} is the umbrella for methods that enable machines to perform tasks associated with intelligence; \ac{ml} is a major subset of \ac{ai} in which systems learn from data rather than relying only on hand-crafted rules; and deep learning is a dominant family of \ac{ml} methods that learn layered representations from large datasets. Within deep learning, \ac{genai} refers to models that can produce new content like text, images, audio, code, and \acp{llm} are generative models specialized in language and often code, typically implemented as neural networks trained to predict and generate sequences of tokens.

\acp{llm} are neural networks, typically transformers, trained to predict the next token in a sequence, essentially probabilistic sequence models \cite{vaswani_AttentionAllYou_2017}. They are first pre-trained on massive text corpora to learn general language patterns, then often fine-tuned with human feedback to better follow user intents \cite{commoncrawl_CommonCrawlOpen_2024, ouyang_TrainingLanguageModels_2022}. This two-stage training is the foundation to \ac{llm} generative abilities. Since \acp{llm} generate text by learned statistical associations rather than a grounded world model, they can produce outputs that sound fluent and confident while being incorrect or inconsistent; in other words, an \ac{llm} may "hallucinate" which is defines as generating plausible-sounding but factually false or nonsensical content \cite{kalai_WhyLanguageModelsHallucinate_2025}. Researchers have characterized \acp{llm} as “stochastic parrots,” adept at regurgitating patterns without true understanding \cite{bender_DangersStochasticParrots_2021}. As a result, consistency and groundedness are not guaranteed: an \ac{llm} might contradict itself or deviate from known facts if such outputs have high probability under its training distribution. These issues raise concerns when applying \acp{llm} in domains that require rigor and trustworthiness. 

\subsubsection{Generative AI Foundations}
\label{subsec:genai_foundations}

\ac{genai} refers to \ac{ml} systems that model a data-generating process well enough to produce new samples that resemble the training distribution, rather than only assigning labels or scores to inputs. In practice, this means the model learns a probability distribution over sequences (for text/code) or over other structured domains (e.g., pixels, audio waveforms), enabling it to synthesize novel outputs conditioned on prompts, partial inputs, or other context. \acp{llm} are the language-specialized instantiation of this idea: they represent a conditional generative model over token sequences, producing continuations that are probable under learned patterns \cite{vaswani_AttentionAllYou_2017}.

A useful distinction for engineering contexts is between training and inference. Training establishes the parameters that encode statistical regularities in the data, while inference is the deployment-time process of producing outputs from a fixed model given a prompt and context window. At inference time, an \ac{llm} does not retrieve a single deterministic "answer"; it samples (or approximately maximizes) from a learned distribution over possible next tokens. The specific decoding strategy (e.g., greedy decoding, beam search, temperature sampling, nucleus/top-$p$ sampling) directly affects determinism, diversity, and error modes. For systems engineering tasks, these decoding choices matter because they change how often a model will introduce novel phrasing, omit details, or drift from constraints when rewriting artifacts.

Modern \acp{llm} are commonly trained and adapted in multiple stages. Self-supervised pretraining learns broad linguistic competence from large corpora by optimizing next-token (or masked-token) prediction objectives \cite{commoncrawl_CommonCrawlOpen_2024}. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) then shapes the model toward task-following behavior using curated input--output examples (e.g., instruction--response pairs). Many production systems add preference-based alignment, where the model is optimized against human (or proxy) preference judgments to reduce harmful behavior and increase helpfulness and instruction adherence. While these stages typically improve usability, they also introduce an important measurement implication: the deployed model is no longer merely a next-token predictor trained on raw text; it is a model whose behavior reflects multiple objective functions and datasets, which can shift failure modes in ways that may not be visible in aggregate benchmark scores.

\ac{genai} systems can also be augmented without modifying weights. Prompting and in-context learning condition the model via examples, formats, and constraints embedded in the input. \ac{rag} conditions outputs on external references fetched at runtime, reducing reliance on parametric memory and enabling citations to authoritative sources. Tool-using and agentic patterns extend this further by allowing the model to call external functions (e.g., search, simulation, model checking, requirements databases), then incorporate results into outputs. \subsubsection{Operational Model Characteristics and Deployment Considerations}
\label{subsec:llm_ops_characteristics}

Several operational characteristics affect how \acp{llm} behave in practice and how they can be integrated into engineering environments. Model size is typically reported as the number of parameters, which is a rough proxy for capacity but not a guarantee of domain performance. Context window length determines how much of an artifact network can be provided at once (e.g., a requirement, its rationale, upstream assumptions, interface definitions, and verification evidence), constraining the degree of holistic consistency the model can maintain in a single pass.

Inference configuration (temperature, top-$p$) controls the diversity--determinism trade and can influence whether edits remain conservative or introduce unintended changes.

Deployment constraints frequently motivate quantization, which compresses model weights into lower-precision representations (e.g., 16-bit, 8-bit, 4-bit) to reduce memory footprint and increase throughput. Quantization can enable local or edge deployment and faster iteration, but it may also change numerical behavior and, in some settings, affect accuracy or reliability . Practically, engineers often interact with models through inference servers and local run times (e.g., GPU-optimized serving stacks for throughput, or lightweight local runners for offline use), which shapes the feasible workflow patterns.

Finally, it is useful to distinguish between \emph{model capability} and \emph{system capability}. Many effective generative AI applications in engineering rely on the surrounding system: structured prompting, retrieval from authoritative baselines, tool calls to analysis engines, and validation steps that check outputs against schemas, constraints, or tests. 

This framing aligns with the digital-thread perspective adopted in this chapter: the model is one component within a configuration-managed pipeline, and its outputs must be treated as proposed changes to artifacts that require integration, verification, and traceability control.

\paragraph{Empirical Studies of AI-Assisted Systems Engineering}\label{par:ch2_empirical_ai_and_se}

Recent empirical work demonstrates both the promise and limitations of LLM-based systems engineering assistance. Controlled experiments on prompt engineering for SE artifact generation reveal that oneshot, fewshot, and chain-of-thought techniques substantially improve quality over zeroshot prompting, though performance varies significantly with temperature and artifact type \cite{SmithCrabb_Jones_Bernard_Prompt_Technique_SE_Artifacts_2024}. When evaluated against INCOSE requirements-writing guidelines, LLM-generated requirements achieve precision of 0.897 and recall of 0.976 for text-based extraction, with quality on par with human SME outputs, though human-in-the-loop validation remains essential \cite{Stein_Leveraging_LLMs_Requirements_Generation_SE_Guidelines_2025}. Fine-tuned models with chain-of-thought prompting significantly outperform baseline approaches on SysMLv2 generation tasks when evaluated using syntax scores, logic scores, MAUVE, and BertScore, demonstrating measurable quality improvements \cite{Wach_LLMs_Accelerate_Complex_Systems_2024}.

Systematic validation of AI-generated systems engineering artifacts requires moving beyond informal review. Syntactic and Semantic Quality (SSQ) and Validity (SSV) metrics enable continuous scoring of SysML model correctness through parser-based validation and simulation feedback, with empirical improvements observed when LLMs are guided by formal grammars rather than free-form prompting \cite{Traveller_Agile_Engineering_Agentic_Co_Modelers_2025}. Multimodal Retrieval-Augmented Generation (M-RAG) approaches that incorporate text, images, and tables outperform GPT-4-only and text-only RAG methods on design document evaluation scores, with structured multi-LLM scoring reducing subjectivity \cite{Guntupalli_Integrating_Generative_AI_Automation_System_Design_Processes_2024}. However, physical feasibility and downstream implementation risks remain incompletely validated, requiring domain-expert review.

\paragraph{Agentic AI architectures for integrated SE workflows.}
Agentic AI systems demonstrate dramatic productivity gains by orchestrating LLMs with engineering tools across the full SE lifecycle. The DaVinci platform reduces system model creation from 100 hours to 20 minutes and generates requirements, architecture, interfaces, behaviors, schedules, and risk matrices in minutes by integrating LLMs with CAD, simulation, and documentation tools \cite{Helmerich_Transforming_Systems_Engineering_Agentic_AI_2025}. Similarly, cognitive-agentic architectures that augment GenAI with verification, simulation, and optimization tools address standalone GenAI's limitations in reliability, explainability, and uncertainty awareness \cite{Lee_CastilloEffen_2024_Rapid_Intelligent_Systems_Engineering}. These approaches position AI not as a replacement for SE tools but as an orchestration layer providing natural-language interfaces to validated engineering capabilities.





\subsection{Systems engineering risks and failure modes for LLM adoption}
\label{subsec:ch2_llm_failure_modes}

The same properties that make LLMs attractive for systems engineering—fluency, synthesis, and transformation across representations—also create distinctive risks when models are used to produce or modify lifecycle artifacts. Many failure modes that are already widely recognized in general-purpose LLM use become more consequential in \ac{se} because they can yield \emph{plausible-but-wrong} artifacts that appear coherent in isolation while degrading traceability, consistency, and auditability across the technical baseline.


\paragraph{Hallucination and fabrication in lifecycle artifacts.}
A foundational risk is hallucination: the generation of statements, constraints, references, or rationale that are not supported by stakeholder intent or authoritative sources. In document-centric settings, such errors may remain hidden because the output reads plausibly. In a digital-thread environment, hallucinations can be operationalized into the baseline: an LLM-generated requirement may introduce an invented constraint or misstate a performance threshold; if accepted without careful redlining and review, that error can propagate into architecture decisions, interface assumptions, verification criteria, and ultimately design commitments. Similar dynamics apply to risk artifacts: hallucinated hazards, mitigations, or evidence statements can distort the risk register and undermine risk-informed decision making. When retrieval is used, hallucination may present as \emph{mis-grounding}—outputs that cite retrieved material but apply it incorrectly, misattribute the source, or infer requirements not actually supported by the referenced text.

\paragraph{Requirements quality degradation through ambiguity and non-verifiability.}
Even when LLM outputs are not factually fabricated, they may degrade requirements quality through ambiguity, non-atomic statements, missing conditions, or unverifiable language. This is especially problematic because these defects are often subtle and are easily mistaken for completeness due to fluent phrasing. Work evaluating LLM-assisted requirements generation against \ac{incose}-aligned requirements-writing criteria demonstrates that domain-specific quality standards are essential and that human review remains necessary even when LLM-generated requirements appear superficially well-formed \cite{Stein_Leveraging_LLMs_Requirements_Generation_SE_Guidelines_2025}. In lifecycle terms, unclear or non-verifiable requirements increase downstream design churn and complicate verification planning by weakening the mapping between intent, implementation, and evidence.

\paragraph{Cross-artifact inconsistency and incomplete propagation under change.}
A second class of risks involves inconsistency across artifacts. In repository-centric baselines, a change to one artifact often requires coordinated updates to others (e.g., requirement $\rightarrow$ architecture constraint $\rightarrow$ verification procedure). LLM-assisted edits that are locally reasonable but incompletely propagated can introduce drift across interfaces, constraints, acceptance criteria, and rationale. Over time, this drift can accumulate as technical debt within the digital thread, increasing rework and undermining confidence in the baseline \cite{Ouzzif_Leveraging_AI_Manage_Technical_Debt_Test_Evaluation_2025}. These risks are amplified in hybrid environments where translation between narrative and structured representations is frequent and where semantic mismatches at representation boundaries can be difficult to detect.

\paragraph{Omission and incompleteness in coupled lifecycle reasoning.}
LLMs can also fail through omission: leaving out critical conditions, dependencies, exceptions, or downstream updates. \paragraph{Instability, non-determinism, and configuration churn.}
A further risk in artifact-centric workflows is instability: semantically different outputs for similar inputs or for repeated runs under slightly different contexts. In systems engineering settings, this can manifest as inconsistent requirement interpretations, shifting risk statements, or conflicting summaries of the same baseline, producing configuration churn and complicating audit and review. When LLMs are used repeatedly as part of ongoing change workflows, instability can degrade confidence in the reproducibility of decisions and rationale, even when each individual output appears plausible.

Finally, LLMs often produce fluent outputs without calibrated uncertainty, which can lead to statements that appear authoritative while violating engineering constraints, organizational standards, or domain guidelines. In model-centric contexts, non-compliance can also include structural violations (e.g., schema mismatches, invalid modeling constructs, or naming convention violations) that are not easily detected by casual reading. Surveyed opportunities and cautions in the systems engineering AI literature emphasize the need to treat LLMs as assistants embedded within socio-technical workflows rather than as autonomous systems engineering agents \cite{gadewadikar_selma_2024}.

Benchmarking is often treated as a way to rank models, but in domain practice it functions as \emph{qualification evidence}: evidence supporting claims about whether a model is suitable for an intended use with known consequences of failure. This section develops that evidence-oriented view and applies it to systems engineering (SE), where errors can propagate across lifecycle decisions and assurance artifacts. The next section analyzes sources of evaluation unreliability, followed by a comparison of evaluation modalities as distinct evidence types and a discussion of cost and computational constraints.


\subsection{Benchmarking in the LLM ecosystem}
\label{subsec:benchmarking_llm_ecosystem}

Large language models (LLMs) are transformer-based neural networks trained to predict and generate sequences of text, an approach popularized by the attention-based architecture that enabled scaling to large corpora and model sizes \cite{vaswani_AttentionAllYou_2017}. In contemporary machine learning practice, LLM \emph{benchmarking} typically refers to evaluating models on standardized datasets and tasks under a defined protocol to (i) compare models, (ii) track progress over time, and (iii) provide selection signals for downstream use \cite{huggingface_open_2024, myrzakhan_open-llm-leaderboard_2024}. Benchmarking is therefore widely treated as a mechanism for establishing shared reference points across rapidly evolving model families.

However, as LLMs move from general demonstrations to professional and safety-relevant use, a central limitation emerges: general-purpose benchmark performance does not reliably translate into evidence of suitability within specialized domains. Domain settings impose their own definitions of correctness, acceptable uncertainty, required provenance, and workflow constraints. These requirements motivate the development of domain-specific benchmarks and evaluation suites in areas such as biomedicine, engineering, law, and finance, where task formulations, grading criteria, and failure costs differ materially from general \ac{nlp} settings \cite{jin_pubmedqa_2019, Jin_Li_2024_Benchmarking_LLMs_EBM, doris_designqa_2024, syed_benchmarking_2024, li_eee-bench_2025, zhou_engibench_2025, fei_LawBenchBenchmarkingLegal_2023, guha_LegalbenchCollaborativelyBuilt_2023, chalkidis_LexGLUEBenchmarkDataset_2022,islam_financebench_2023}. Evidence from specialized benchmarking efforts suggests that strong performance on broad or generic evaluations does not guarantee robust performance on domain-specific tasks, particularly when domain terminology or domain workflows are central to correctness \cite{anjum_DomainSpecificBenchmarks_2025}.

This dissertation adopts that same principle in the systems engineering (SE) domain. Rather than treating benchmark scores as portable measures of general capability, the goal is to treat evaluation results as \emph{qualification evidence} whose meaning depends on intended use, domain constraints, and the consequences of failure. The remainder of this section therefore re-frames benchmarking in evidence-oriented terms and then specializes the argument to SE, where lifecycle coupling, traceability expectations, and artifact-network propagation make the adequacy of evidence particularly sensitive to evaluation design.

% The core premise of this section is that evaluation must be grounded in the structure of SE work products and the assurance expectations that govern them. When an LLM is used to draft requirements, transform interface definitions, summarize verification results, or propose trade-offs, the dominant risk is not merely that the model occasionally makes mistakes, but that it makes mistakes that are difficult to detect and that propagate across a coupled artifact set. This motivates a literature review that is organized around (i) the nature of SE artifacts and lifecycle coupling, (ii) how LLM capabilities and failure modes interact with those artifacts, and (iii) why evaluation pipelines can distort evidence through dataset lifecycle hazards, reproducibility sensitivity, and modality-dependent measurement effects.


\subsection{Benchmarks as Measurement Instruments}
\label{subsec:benchmarks_as_instruments}

A benchmark is a measurement instrument that formalizes domain knowledge, reasoning, and instruction-following through task design, dataset composition, and a scoring rule. The resulting score is not a deployment guarantee; it is a conditional measurement produced under a specific protocol. In other words, benchmark results are only as meaningful as the assumptions that connect the measured behavior to the intended use.

This instrument view aligns with broader quality and risk perspectives in engineering: measurement must be interpreted in context, and higher-stakes applications require stronger justification that the measurement reflects the property of interest \cite{iso_iec_25010_2011, nist_ai_rmf_2023}. For \ac{se}, the central question is not "Which model ranks highest?" but "What claims about suitability for intended use does this evidence support, and what important failure modes might it miss?"

\subsection{Benchmarking as qualification evidence in systems engineering}
\label{subsec:benchmarking_as_qualification_evidence_se}

In systems engineering, \emph{qualification evidence} refers to evidence sufficient to support bounded claims that a capability is suitable for a specified intended use under defined constraints and consequence expectations. This notion is familiar in SE assurance practice: confidence is built by aligning evidence to what must remain true of lifecycle work products and by demonstrating that the system (or capability) behaves acceptably under relevant conditions and uncertainties \cite{iso_ieee_15288_2015, incose_se_handbook_2023}.

Benchmarking becomes qualification-relevant in SE because LLM outputs are rarely consumed in isolation. Instead, they are introduced into lifecycle workflows and technical baselines that include requirements, interfaces, design descriptions, trade studies, verification artifacts, rationale, and risk management products. In digital engineering and \ac{mbse} environments, these work products are increasingly linked through a digital thread, and their internal consistency and traceability are part of what makes the baseline auditable and governable \cite{dod_digital_engineering_strategy_2018, estefanSurveyModelBasedSystems2008}. Errors introduced upstream can therefore propagate downstream: an unclear or incorrect requirement can bias architecture decisions and verification planning; an omitted constraint can remain latent until integration; a mischaracterized risk can distort mitigation priorities. The consequence profile is not occasional wrong text but lifecycle drift that can become embedded in authoritative artifacts.

This shifts what benchmark evidence must do. The most consequential failures are often those that (i) appear plausible at the point of creation, (ii) evade detection during routine review, and (iii) influence downstream engineering decisions. In SE terms, these are \emph{latent and propagative} defects. They may not present as obviously incorrect prose; they may present as missing assumptions, incomplete updates across lifecycle products, or content that is locally coherent but globally misaligned with the evolving baseline. This is why qualification-oriented benchmarking emphasizes not only average correctness but also the ability of an evaluation to \emph{expose} the failure modes that matter for the intended use and consequence class \cite{amodeiConcreteProblemsAI2016, parker_automation_bias_2019}.

Within this dissertation, the evidence-oriented view of benchmarking is formalized through a systems-engineering-specific benchmark (SysEngBench) \cite{bell_IntroducingSysEngBenchNovel_2024, bell_rabellsysengbench_2024, Bell2025SysEngBench}. However, the argument in this section is broader: any evaluation regime intended to support SE adoption decisions must be interpreted as qualification evidence, meaning it must justify why the measured behavior supports claims about intended use in SE workflows rather than assuming that generic benchmark success transfers automatically.

\subsection{Governance as the Forcing Function for Evidence}
\label{subsec:governance_forcing_function}

Governance frameworks make the ``benchmarking as qualification evidence'' framing non-optional. In federal and defense contexts, risk management and policy guidance increasingly require that AI systems be governed according to intended use, consequence, and lifecycle risk. The \ac{nist} AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) explicitly frames AI risk as socio-technical and lifecycle-spanning, emphasizing governance, contextual mapping, measurement, and ongoing management rather than one-time validation \cite{nist_ai_rmf_2023}. Policy instruments similarly push agencies toward inventorying AI uses, applying risk-based safeguards, and documenting evidence commensurate with impacts \cite{Office_of_Management_and_Budget_2024_M24-10_AI_Governance, Department_of_Homeland_Security_2023_AI_Acquisition_Use}.

SE-facing assurance efforts extend this logic into concrete practice: emphasizing lifecycle-aligned assurance activities, repeatable test and evaluation strategies, and reusable assurance artifacts (e.g., hazard analyses and traceability-oriented evidence packages) \cite{Johnson_Operationalizing_AI_Assurance_2025, Lanus_Tailorable_Risk_Informed_AI_Test_Evaluation_Strategy_2025, Charnetzki_Hazard_Analysis_RAG_LLM_Systems_2025}. The same underlying AI capability can exhibit different performance with risk tradeoffs depending on how it is integrated into human workflows and engineering processes, motivating workflow-aware assessment rather than isolated model scoring \cite{Szajnfarber_SERC_Perspective_AI4SE_SE4AI_2025, heckle2024responsible}. Collectively, these governance and assurance perspectives reinforce a central implication for this dissertation: benchmark scores can contribute to an assurance argument, but only when treated as bounded, contextual evidence aligned to intended use, failure consequences, and lifecycle controls.

Even when benchmarking is framed correctly as qualification evidence, the evidence can be distorted if the evaluation itself is unreliable. In practice, results can vary due to dataset lifecycle hazards (e.g., leakage and contamination), protocol sensitivity, and reproducibility limits; these effects can produce measurements that appear stable while masking uncertainty about what a benchmark actually captured \cite{magar2023data, dodge2021documenting, lucas2023evaluating}. For SE adoption decisions, this matters because unreliable measurement can yield misleading assurance signals: a score may look reassuring while failing to expose latent, propagative defects that dominate lifecycle risk. The next section therefore examines mechanisms that make LLM evaluation unreliable before comparing evaluation modalities as distinct evidence types and addressing cost and computational constraints.

\paragraph{Benchmark, task, and metric.}
A \textbf{benchmark} is a set of inputs (prompts, questions, documents), reference outputs or evaluation criteria, and an interaction protocol defining how a model is queried and scored. A \textbf{task} is what the model is asked to do (e.g., answer a question, summarize a document, generate requirements). A \textbf{metric} is the scoring rule that maps outputs to a quantitative value (e.g., accuracy, BLEU, ROUGE). This distinction matters because a benchmark can be stable while the \textit{metric} is not valid for the task, or a metric can be well-defined while the benchmark items contain exploitable artifacts.

\paragraph{Training, adaptation, and inference.}
We use \textbf{pre-training} to denote large-scale training on broad corpora to learn general language structure and world knowledge; \textbf{domain-adaptive pre-training} (DAPT) to denote further pre-training on domain-relevant corpora; \textbf{fine-tuning} to denote supervised adaptation for task performance; and \textbf{inference} to denote model execution on inputs . These distinctions matter because benchmark contamination risks differ depending on whether a model has seen benchmark items during pre-training, fine-tuning, instruction tuning, or evaluation-time retrieval.

\paragraph{Model and quantization choices.}
Model characteristics such as parameter count and quantization (e.g., 16-bit, 8-bit, 4-bit) alter cost/latency and can shift behavior in ways that complicate cross-study comparability. Likewise, decoding parameters (e.g., temperature) change output stochasticity; qualification-oriented evaluations commonly set temperature to zero (or otherwise constrain decoding) to reduce randomness and support reproducibility, but this does not eliminate all sources of variance (e.g., nondeterminism in serving stacks, tool calls, or judge models).

\paragraph{Scope note: multimodal and "spatial" models.}
While the broader foundation model ecosystem increasingly targets multimodal perception and spatial reasoning, this dissertation’s evaluation scope is \textbf{textual} tasks and artifact-centered textual work products. Recent industry framing describes "spatial intelligence" as a next frontier beyond language-only competence \cite{world-labs_manifesto_spatial_2025}. That trajectory motivates future SE evaluation expansion, but the present work focuses on text-based benchmarking evidence relevant to systems engineering artifacts and documentation.

\acp{mcq} have long been a mainstay in educational and professional assessments due to their simplicity and objectivity. In the context of evaluating \acp{llm}, \acp{mcq} offer a straightforward testing mechanism: a model selects from a finite set of answers, making it easy to measure correctness. The \ac{mcq} format provides a quantifiable snapshot of a model’s baseline knowledge and pattern-recognition abilities.

However, \acp{mcq} inherently conceal the complexity of the reasoning processes that can be assessed, meaning they offer little insight into how or why a model arrived at a particular choice. For systems engineering, the how or why is critical to evaluating competing trade-spaces and picking a solution that balances competing interests. 

By constraining possible answers, \acp{mcq} may oversimplify complex topics. Systems engineering scenarios that require in-depth reasoning, synthesis of multiple concepts, or consideration of subtle trade-offs are not easily captured by a handful of fixed options. This lack of nuance means MCQs may not truly reflect the model’s capacity for deep understanding or its ability to articulate reasoning processes 
\cite{zheng_large_2024, fu_mcq_reasoning_more_confident_when_wrong_2025}. In complex engineering environments, relying solely on MCQs risks obtaining an incomplete picture of the model’s actual capabilities. Recent research suggests that providing \acp{llm} with no correct \ac{mcq} can assist in measuring reasoning and critical thinking \cite{goral_when_all_options_are_wrong_2024}.

Textual response formats challenge LLMs to generate answers in free form, demanding a richer comprehension of the underlying material. By forgoing multiple-choice constraints, these formats encourage models to articulate reasoning, structure their responses coherently, and demonstrate the ability to connect concepts holistically. For instance, a prompt might ask the LLM to explain how a particular subsystem interacts with other components or to propose a solution that balances performance and cost.

The advantage of textual responses lies in their ability to reveal more about the model’s internal “thought process” as demonstrated by reasoning models like ChatGPT o1 or DeepSeek R1 \cite{IntroducingOpenAIO1_2024, deepseekai_deepseekR1-2025}. Evaluating free text answers can uncover subtle reasoning errors or expose areas where the model lacks conceptual depth. While more complex to assess automatically, textual response evaluation could be aided by sophisticated metrics or even human expert review.

Despite their merits, textual responses pose significant practical challenges. Creating a dataset of open-ended questions is often more involved than formulating MCQs. It requires careful thought to ensure that prompts are unambiguous and representative of real-world tasks, while maintaining a relatively tightly bounded question. Since textual responses are not strictly right or wrong in the same way MCQ choices are, developing reliable evaluation metrics is complex. Traditional measures like accuracy scores do not directly apply. The model might produce grammatically correct but conceptually vacuous answers or resort to vague generalities that sound authoritative but lack technical rigor \cite{fu_mcq_reasoning_more_confident_when_wrong_2025,Sourav_Banerjee_2024_Vulnerability_LLM_Benchmarks}. Addressing these challenges will involve combining automated metrics with domain expert human evaluation.

\subsubsection{Evidence fusion for risk-aware qualification (new synthesis)}
\label{subsec:ch2_evidence_fusion}

No single evaluation modality provides sufficient evidence for qualifying an LLM for SE use. MCQs can be treated as low-cost, high-coverage probes of recognition and constrained reasoning but require robustness checks to validate measurement stability. OSQs provide higher-fidelity evidence for synthesis and justification but require bounded formats, explicit rubrics, and repeatable judging protocols. LLM-as-judge methods can scale OSQ evaluation, but only when their validity is supported by calibration sets, multiple judges, and bias checks.

In this dissertation, evidence fusion is treated as a practical strategy for risk-aware qualification: MCQ robustness analysis reduces the risk of overconfidence in brittle scores; OSQ evaluation provides richer evidence for tasks that resemble SE work; and judge calibration methods provide a path to scalable evaluation while preserving interpretability. Chapters~3--6 operationalize this by designing paired MCQ/OSQ items, testing robustness under distractor variation, and analyzing the cost and evidence trade-offs of modality selection.

\subsection{Dataset lifecycle hazards}
\label{subsec:ch2_dataset_lifecycle_hazards}

Benchmarks are not static objects: they are created, documented, released, reused, and eventually become stale if not maintained. Lifecycle hazards accumulate over time and can systematically inflate or distort measured performance.


\paragraph{Creation choices shape what can be measured.}
Benchmark construction methods include expert authoring, crowdsourcing, web mining, gamification, automated synthesis, and adaptation from existing resources; these are often combined \cite{davisBenchmarksAutomatedCommonsense_2023}. Each choice affects item quality, difficulty calibration, and what the benchmark is actually sensitive to. For example, item defects (incorrect labels, multiple correct answers, ambiguous wording) and unnatural language can confound evaluation and invite shortcut learning \cite{davisBenchmarksAutomatedCommonsense_2023}. Difficulty design also matters: Bloom-style mappings and cognitive complexity frameworks can help align item difficulty with intended constructs \cite{blooms_taxonomy_1956, Liangtai_Sun_2023_SciEval_Benchmark, R_Sujay_2024_Question_Complexity_Estimation}.

\paragraph{Contamination, saturation, and decay.}
Three lifecycle hazards are particularly consequential:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Contamination:} benchmark exposure during training or tuning can inflate scores via memorization or leakage. Recent work distinguishes contamination types and proposes detection methods beyond naive string matching \cite{Cheng_2024_Benchmark_Data_Contamination, oren_black_box_contamination_2023, yang_rethinking_2023, sainz_nlp_2023}. Statistical approaches also broaden ``contamination'' to include any inflated, non-generalizing performance and attempt to quantify the effect using reference benchmarks and model sets \cite{Dekoninck_2024_ConStat}.
  \item \textbf{Saturation:} once most models achieve near-ceiling performance, the benchmark loses discriminative power and can mask important differences (e.g., robustness, reasoning faithfulness).
  \item \textbf{Decay (deprecation pressure):} as tasks drift away from stakeholder-relevant work, benchmarks can become actively misleading unless versioned, retired, or scoped appropriately. Emerging work argues for explicit criteria and processes to deprecate benchmarks that no longer serve their purpose \cite{Ayrton_San_Joaquin_2025_Deprecating_Benchmarks}.
\end{itemize}

Contamination is not purely binary (seen/unseen). Benchmark Data Contamination (BDC) frameworks characterize levels from semantic/topic exposure to full label exposure, emphasizing that even metadata and public analyses of a benchmark can indirectly bias evaluations \cite{Cheng_2024_Benchmark_Data_Contamination}. Additional perspectives examine when contamination effects diminish as training data grows (``forgetting'' dynamics), complicating simplistic causal attributions from model behavior to single datapoints \cite{Bordt_2024_Forgetting_Data_Contamination}. Multilingual settings introduce further leakage pathways because translations and parallel corpora blur dataset boundaries \cite{Ahuja_2024_Multilingual_Benchmark_Contamination}.

\subsection{Protocol and tooling sensitivity}
\label{subsec:ch2_protocol_tooling_sensitivity}

Even when a dataset is well-formed, evaluation results can be highly sensitive to the protocol and toolchain: prompt templates, few-shot formatting, decoding constraints, harness defaults, and scoring rules. For qualification, this sensitivity is itself a measurement property: it determines whether a score is reproducible and interpretable.

\paragraph{Harness effects and implementation degrees of freedom.}
LLM evaluation is frequently executed via standardized harnesses and benchmark runners (e.g., lm-eval-style frameworks \cite{eval-harness}, HELM suites \cite{liang_HolisticEvaluationLanguage_2023}) that encode many "silent" decisions: answer extraction logic, normalization rules, stop sequences, sampling policies, and dataset version pinning. Small differences can yield nontrivial score deltas, especially on multiple-choice tasks (where parsing and option formatting matter) and on open-form tasks (where rubric and grader prompts matter). Critiques of benchmarking practice highlight that such degrees of freedom contribute to irreproducible or non-comparable claims across papers \cite{mcintosh_inadequacies_2024, laskar_systematic_2024}.

\paragraph{Evaluation infrastructure is now productized.}
Modern evaluation stacks increasingly resemble software testing ecosystems: unit tests for LLM outputs, regression suites, CI integration, and ``eval observability'' platforms. For example, open-source frameworks position LLM evaluation as pytest-like testing with configurable metrics and judge models \cite{confidentai_deepeval_2026}. This trend is valuable for operational discipline, but it also increases the surface area of measurement assumptions: metric definitions, judge selection, prompt templates, and versioning policies become part of the evidence trail.

\paragraph{Design implication: treat evaluation as a controlled experiment.}
For benchmarking evidence to function as qualification evidence, protocols must be fully specified (dataset version, prompt template, decoding parameters, grader model + rubric, scoring code revision) and evaluated for sensitivity. Where sensitivity is high, confidence intervals and repeated trials (or perturbation tests) are more informative than point estimates.

\subsection{Scoring instability and evaluator bias}
\label{subsec:ch2_scoring_instability_evaluator_bias}

Scoring needs to be treated as a second measurement instrument layered onto model outputs.

\paragraph{Metric failure modes.}
Automatic metrics can be gamed or can systematically under-measure qualities stakeholders care about (e.g., semantic adequacy, groundedness, traceability). The BLEU lineage illustrates how optimizing overlap can miss meaning and encourage superficial strategies \cite{papineni_bleu_2002}. In modern LLM settings, analogous effects arise when models optimize for benchmark-specific artifacts or when reward models and graders incentivize ``good-looking'' answers over faithful ones.

\paragraph{Open-form scoring introduces grader variance.}
Open-style questions improve construct coverage (reasoning, assumptions, traceability), but scoring introduces subjectivity. Human grading yields high fidelity but is costly and can suffer from inter-rater disagreement. LLM-as-judge approaches offer scalability, yet must be validated for stability, bias, and calibration \cite{zheng2023judging, liu2023gpt, kuo2024llmjudge}. Without such validation, judge-based scores can become circular (a model evaluating models) and can encode systematic preference for certain styles, verbosity, or phrasing.

\paragraph{Bias and instability propagate through pipelines.}
Pipeline-level bias can be introduced by:
(i) judge model selection (and its training data),
(ii) rubric prompt wording,
(iii) few-shot exemplars used to "teach" the judge,
(iv) aggregation choices (mean vs. median vs. trimmed mean),
and (v) selective reporting (only best-performing protocol variants). For qualification, these must be treated as auditable configuration, not incidental details.

Qualification evidence is not free: evidence depth and reliability depend on the cost of running models, generating responses, and, in \ac{osq} settings, judging outputs. These costs impose feasibility constraints on evaluation pipelines, shaping practical choices about sample size, replication, modality mix, response-length policy, and scoring strategy. \paragraph{Cost as an Evaluation Design Constraint}
% Trade-space awareness mostly
Evaluation for intended use is often discussed in terms of validity and reliability, such as whether an instrument measures what it claims to measure and whether it does so consistently. In practice, evaluation is also constrained by feasibility: compute access, wall clock time, and usage based pricing determine how much evidence can be collected and how robustly it can be tested. These constraints are essential for LLM evaluation because costs scale with input context length, output length, and the number of repeated runs required for robustness (e.g. prompt variants) \cite{li_llms_as_judges_2024, lu2024bias}. For \acp{osq}, feasibility is further shaped by scoring overhead: the cost of human grading or the additional inference required for LLM-as-a-judge pipelines can dominate the cost budget that research is looking to curb \cite{huang_LLMasaJudgeScalableTest_2025, huang_LLMasaJudgeScalableTest_2025, tang_LearningEfficientMultiTurn_2026}. As a result, evaluation design becomes a trade-space problem in which breadth (more items, more topics) competes with depth (richer responses, artifact-proximal tasks) and reliability (replication and stability checks) competes with throughput.

\paragraph{Systems Engineering Cost Modeling as Conceptual Grounding}
Cost trade-space reasoning is a familiar theme in systems engineering. Parametric models such as \ac{cocomo} estimate software development cost using drivers related to size, complexity, and personnel factors \cite{boehm_cocomo_1981}. Analogously, \ac{cosysmo} extends parametric estimation to systems engineering effort by accounting for system complexity, integration, and related drivers \cite{valerdi_cosysmo_2005}. More broadly, the systems engineering cost estimation ecosystem includes a range of approaches such as SEER and PRICE tools and hybrid parametric-analogy models that are used to support planning and decision-making under uncertainty \cite{madachy_systems_2024, madachy_cost_estimation_2014}. The relevance of these foundations in the present context is conceptual rather than one-to-one: LLM evaluation introduces different primitives (tokens, latency, and judge overhead), but the underlying logic is similar. When evidence must be collected under constraints, cost-aware trade-space reasoning is necessary to select feasible evaluation regimes and to interpret results in light of the evidence that could practically be gathered. Efforts have already begun to define the cost modeling factors necessary for \ac{ai} augmented efforts \cite{madachy_SystemsAcquisitionCost_2024, Madachy_CostModelingAI_2024, madachy_GenerativeAIdrivenSystems_2025}

\paragraph{Inference costs and the marginal cost of evidence collection.}
In contrast to training, inference costs scale with usage and therefore often dominate total cost of ownership for widely deployed systems. For evaluation programs, inference cost is the primary driver of feasibility because it scales directly with the number of items, the number of models, and the number of repeated runs used for robustness. Inference cost is determined by (i) the number of tokens processed in the input context, (ii) the number of tokens generated in the output, and (iii) the serving characteristics of the system (e.g., batching efficiency, memory footprint, and key--value cache behavior). Tokenomics provides a vocabulary for these serving constraints through throughput (tokens/sec), cost (e.g., USD per million tokens), and quality-of-experience metrics such as time to first token (TTFT), time per output token (TPOT), and end-to-end latency \cite{dong_large_2024}. These measures translate to evaluation feasibility by determining how quickly and how expensively an experiment grid (items $\times$ conditions $\times$ replications) can be executed.

Inference cost is also shaped by the evaluation protocol itself. Robustness checks (multiple seeds, prompt or format variants, controlled structural changes, or multiple samples) multiply inference calls. For OSQs, scoring can introduce an additional inference layer when LLM-as-a-judge pipelines are used: the judge prompt often includes the candidate response, rubric text, and sometimes reference answers; stability improvements may require multiple judge passes, further increasing marginal cost. This yields a practical asymmetry between modalities: MCQ evidence is typically inexpensive to score and therefore supports broad coverage, whereas OSQ evidence can be richer but is often gated by scoring overhead. These feasibility pressures motivate compute-aware comparisons; budget-aware evaluation work shows that methods can appear superior primarily because they expend more compute, and conclusions can change when compute is normalized \cite{Junlin_Wang_2024_Budget_Aware_Reasoning}. In applied settings, inference economics is further influenced by access mode: organizations frequently evaluate through LLM-as-a-Service offerings where pricing tiers, throughput constraints, and latency targets shape both marginal cost and scalability \cite{liagkou_cost_2024}. Finally, surveys of resource-efficient foundation models emphasize the importance of measuring efficiency alongside accuracy using metrics such as memory footprint, latency, throughput, energy, and cost per token \cite{xu_resource_efficient_2024, bai_beyond_efficiency_2024}, reinforcing that feasibility constraints can determine how much evaluation evidence can practically be collected even when cost is not itself used as a qualification decision rule.

\section{Synthesis of Literature Findings}
\label{sec:ch2_synthesis}

This chapter reviewed prior work relevant to LLM evaluation in systems engineering and related domains. The literature indicates that SE work products form a coupled artifact network that must remain traceable, auditable, and consistent across lifecycle phases. Prior studies also show that while LLMs can assist with extraction, drafting, and transformation of SE artifacts, known failure modes can yield plausible-but-wrong outputs and cross-artifact inconsistencies that propagate downstream. In addition, evaluation results may be influenced by dataset lifecycle hazards and by sensitivity to tooling, prompting, and scoring procedures.

These observations motivate the dissertation’s research questions and methodological choices. First, because MCQs are commonly used for scalable evaluation yet are sensitive to option structure, the dissertation examines MCQ robustness under distractor and structure variation as a measurement property mentioned in Research Question \ref{RQ1}. Second, because MCQ and OSQ modalities provide different forms of evidence, the dissertation investigates how paired MCQ/OSQ evaluations differ in the evidence they produce and how they can be combined for task-aligned, risk-aware qualification mentioned in Research Question \ref{RQ2}. Third, because evidence depth depends on computational and judging overhead, the dissertation analyzes how modality selection and response length affect computational cost and token efficiency mentioned in Research Question \ref{RQ3}. Chapter \ref{ch:materials_methods} formalizes these questions through an experimental design and analysis pipeline, specifying task formulations, controlled variants, scoring procedures, and cost accounting approaches used in the remainder of the dissertation.
