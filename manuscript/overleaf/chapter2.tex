\chapter{Literature Review}\label{ch:literature_review}

This chapter situates the dissertation at the intersection of systems engineering practice, model-based systems engineering, and large language models, with the posture that if large language models are to be used in systems engineering workflows, then their qualification must be grounded in evidence that matches the kinds of work products and risks that systems engineering actually carries.

It first establishes the systems engineering context in which model qualification matters, emphasizing the role of complexity, lifecycle coupling, and system-of-systems characteristics that amplify consequence when errors propagate across artifacts and stakeholders. 

Followed by the contrast of document-centric engineering with model-centric workflows, highlighting how the digital thread changes what it means to generate, inspect, and verify engineering work products. 

The chapter then reviews LLMs capabilities and limitations for failure modes: hallucination, weak grounding, inconsistent reasoning under small perturbations, and sensitivity to context and prompting. These properties motivate why benchmark score alone cannot be treated as a single interchangeable proxy for deployable competence. 

The second half of the chapter focuses on evaluation practice. It surveys the evolution of modern benchmarking, including known failure modes such as data leakage, contamination, and dataset lifecycle challenges. It then turns to evaluation modalities, comparing and contrasting multiple-choice and open-ended formats and how they measure different facets of capability and produce different kinds of evidence. 

Across these bodies of work, the reviewed literature demonstrates substantial activity in artificial intelligence for systems engineering and in large language model benchmarking more broadly. However, it does not report a benchmark or evaluation framework explicitly designed to support qualification-oriented assessment of LLMs for systems engineering tasks. Existing benchmarks are largely domain-general, and prior AI-for-SE studies typically evaluate task performance without establishing reusable, reproducible evidence suitable for qualification or comparison across models and time \cite{serc_AI4SESE4AIWorkshop_2024, SERC_AI4SE_SE4AI_Research_Application_Workshop_Report_2025}.

This literature review therefore establishes the conceptual requirements for a benchmark-informed qualification approach in systems engineering, and it prepares the methodological foundations for the benchmark design, experimental protocol, and analysis developed in that chapters that follow.

This section motivates why \ac{se} constitutes a domain-specific environment for evaluating \acp{llm}. Systems engineering outputs are artifact-based and lifecycle-coupled: work products must remain configuration-managed, traceable, and auditable across a digital thread. The goal is not to claim that transfer from general-purpose benchmarks is impossible, but to argue that general benchmark performance alone is insufficient qualification evidence for \ac{se} intended use \cite{walden_systems_engineering_handbook_2015, standard_ieee_15288_2023, deputyassistantsecretaryofdefense_DepartmentDefenseDigitalStrategy_2018}

To connect benchmarking to intended-use qualification in \ac{se}, the narrative is developed in three steps. First, describing the artifacts that constitute the systems engineering process and the implications of cross-artifact consistency for any automated assistance. Next, situating the discussion within the digital thread and \ac{mbse} transition, where models are more query-able, and more tightly coupled under configuration control. Finally, show how scale and system-of-systems complexity increase the consequence of error propagation, motivating stronger assurance expectations than those implied by general-purpose and benchmark scores.



\subsection{The Nature of Systems Engineering Artifacts}
\label{subsec:ch2_se_artifacts}

Systems engineering is commonly defined as a disciplined, integrative approach for designing, realizing, and operating complex systems across the full lifecycle. This definition emphasizes both technical integration and governance across time: \ac{se} coordinates diverse specialties into a coherent system that satisfies stakeholder needs within technical, cost, schedule, and risk constraints
\cite{incose_GuideSystemsEngineering_2025, maier_ArtSystemsArchitecting_2002, walden_systems_engineering_handbook_2015}. \ac{se} is a socio-technical practice in which decisions must be explainable to stakeholders, defensible under review, and revisable under evolving constraints \cite{BuedeMiller_EngineeringDesignOfSystems_2016, grady_SystemSynthesisProduct_2010, Blanchard_Fabrycky_Systems_Engineering_and_Analysis_2011, blanchard_system_engineering_management_2008 , Langford_engineering_systems_integration_2012}.

\ac{se} produces interdependent lifecycle work products including requirements, architectures, interface definitions, verification artifacts, trade studies, risk registers, and decision rationale whose quality is evaluated not only on a per-artifact basis, but also through cross-artifact consistency and traceability. Each work product is expected to link upstream intent to downstream verification and to evolve under change control \cite{standard_ieee_15288_2023, walden_systems_engineering_handbook_2015}. In a digital-thread, these links are meant to support audit, impact analysis, and assurance arguments across the lifecycle \cite{deputyassistantsecretaryofdefense_DepartmentDefenseDigitalStrategy_2018, Ouzzif_Leveraging_AI_Manage_Technical_Debt_Test_Evaluation_2025, Helmerich_Transforming_Systems_Engineering_Agentic_AI_2025}.


When \acp{llm} are inserted into \ac{se} workflows, the qualification question becomes whether the model can support drafting, extraction, transformation, and consistency-checking tasks without breaking this artifact network. In other words, the primary evaluation target is often not a single answer, but whether the model preserves or improves traceability, avoids introducing inconsistencies across artifacts, and produces outputs that remain verifiable within the digital thread \cite{NIST_ArtificialIntelligenceRisk_2023,  Traveller_Agile_Engineering_Agentic_Co_Modelers_2025, Ouzzif_Leveraging_AI_Manage_Technical_Debt_Test_Evaluation_2025}.

\subsubsection{From Document-Centric to Model-Centric Baselines}
\label{subsubsec:ch2_doc_to_model}

Historically, systems engineering practice has been dominated by document-centric approaches in which the system definition is distributed across collections of narrative artifacts, including requirements specifications, interface control documents, design descriptions, and verification plans. These artifacts are typically maintained as human-readable files including but not limited to: PDFs, Word documents, spreadsheets, and slide decks often stored in loosely coupled repositories. In such environments, traceability, consistency checking, and configuration control are largely enforced through manual processes: engineers update multiple documents by hand, maintain trace matrices explicitly, and rely on review discipline to detect contradictions and omissions. As system complexity increases, this mode of operation makes cross-artifact coherence increasingly difficult to sustain, and ambiguity regarding the authoritative system baseline becomes more likely \cite{incose_GuideSystemsEngineering_2025}.

Digital engineering and Model-Based Systems Engineering (\ac{mbse}) emerged in response to these limitations. \ac{mbse} is commonly defined as a paradigm that uses formalized representations of systems—models—to support the performance of systems engineering tasks throughout the system lifecycle, in contrast to legacy approaches \cite{incose_GuideSystemsEngineering_2025}. The \ac{sebok} similarly defines \ac{mbse} as "a paradigm that uses formalized representations of systems, known as models, to support and facilitate the performance of systems engineering tasks throughout a system’s life cycle'' \cite{incose_GuideSystemsEngineering_2025}. The central distinction is not simply the use of modeling tools, but the treatment of system definition as structured, governed data rather than as loosely synchronized artifacts.

In model- and repository-centric baselines, system elements are instantiated within a shared repository and rendered across multiple views depending on the framework \cite{OMG_UAF_1.3_2025, chiefinformationofficer_DoDAFArchitectureFramework_2010, zachman_ZachmanFrameworkEnterprise_2003}, but typically include structure and behavior derived from a common underlying model. When attributes change, inter-related analyses and views can update as alternate projections of the same data, reducing fragmentation and the need for manual reconciliation. Reported benefits of this approach include improved traceability, automated consistency checking, and enhanced stakeholder communication, particularly when models function as a single, governed source of truth that can be queried and transformed across lifecycle activities \cite{hart_IntroductionModelBasedSystem_2015}. This shift re-frames the technical baseline from a collection of documents to an interconnected artifact network maintained under configuration control.

At the same time, the literature consistently cautions against viewing \ac{mbse} as a panacea, arguing that poorly implemented \ac{mbse} initiatives often mirror and amplify existing deficiencies in systems engineering practice rather than correcting them, effectively "bastardizing" document-based workflows within a modeling environment \cite{lerat_ThreeReasonsWhy_2010, madni_HandbookModelBasedSystems_2023}.

\subsubsection{Transition Drivers and the Hybrid Reality}
\label{subsubsec:ch2_transition_drivers}

The transition from document-centric to model-centric \ac{se} is driven by a combination of technical, organizational, and strategic pressures. Increasing system complexity—particularly software intensity, interconnectedness, and long lifecycle spans—has made manual synchronization across heterogeneous artifacts increasingly untenable. At the same time, budget constraints and compressed delivery schedules have heightened the need for approaches that reduce rework, improve change impact analysis, and support earlier detection of integration issues.

Institutional digital engineering initiatives reflect these pressures to operate under increasingly restrictive budgets and aggressive delivery timelines, noting that legacy approaches are too slow to keep pace with adversaries \cite{NAVWAR_DE_Strategy_2023}.

Similar motivations underlie digital twin efforts in aerospace and defense, which emphasize the integration of physics-based models, operational data, and historical records to create authoritative digital representations of systems across their lifecycles. digital twin concepts that integrate high-fidelity simulation with health management and fleet data to improve safety and reliability \cite{glaessgen_DigitalTwinParadigmFuture_2012}, while Air Force is conducting efforts to develop a digital thread and digital twin analytical framework that merges modeling and experimental data to support lifecycle decision-making \cite{kraft_AirForceDigital_2016}.

Despite these strategic drivers, most organizations operate in a hybrid state rather than a fully model-centric one. Portions of the engineering baseline may reside in structured repositories with models, requirements databases, code, and configuration items, while other portions remain document-centric, including specifications, contractual deliverables, and slide-based decision artifacts.

The adoption of \ac{mbse} is frequently impeded by various barriers to entry, including training requirements, organizational inertia, tool cost, and difficulties integrating with legacy systems. As a result, organizations often pursue incremental transition strategies rather than wholesale replacement of document-based workflows \cite{systems_engineering_guidebook_2021, vaneman_mbse_demystified_2025}. One contributing factor to this high barrier to entry is the large number of competing frameworks, languages, methods, and tools, which increases the complexity of adoption and proficiency development \cite{OMG_UAF_1.3_2025,
chiefinformationofficer_DoDAFArchitectureFramework_2010,
zachman_ZachmanFrameworkEnterprise_2003, OMG_UML_2_5_1_2017, LML_Specification_2_0_2025, OMG_UPDM_2_1_2016, OMG_KerML_1_0_2025, OMG_BPMN_2_0_2_2014, Dassault_Cameo_Systems_Modeler_2025, Eclipse_Capella_Project_Capella_2026, Ansys_System_Architecture_Modeler_SAM_2026, IBM_Engineering_Rhapsody_2026, Spec_Innovations_Innoslate_2026, OpenMBEE_OpenMBEE_2026, PTC_PTC_Modeler_2026, MathWorks_System_Composer_2026}.

This hybrid reality is further complicated by ongoing standards and tooling transitions. Guidance on transitioning from SysML v1 to SysML v2 highlights the planning, migration, and interoperability challenges organizations must manage as modeling languages and ecosystems evolve \cite{OUSDRE_SysMLV1SysML2_2024, friedenthal_MovingForwardSysMLV2_2024, zhang_MBSE20More_2025}.


\subsubsection{Tooling for the Digital Thread}
\label{subsubsec:ch2_tool_for_digital_thread}

As systems engineering baselines become more structured and repository-managed, the scope for tool augmentation expands beyond drafting isolated documents to operating directly on artifact networks. In digital-thread-enabled environments, tools may support querying, transforming, reconciling, and propagating changes across linked lifecycle artifacts rather than generating narrative content \cite{kumar_AIEnabledDigital_2023}. This shift reflects the underlying premise of the digital thread: value is created by maintaining coherence across the artifact network under change.

Recent work has explored the use of knowledge graphs as a foundation for digital thread architectures, enabling explicit representation of relationships among requirements, design elements, verification activities, and operational data. Agentic-AI-enabled digital thread architectures have been proposed that leverage knowledge graphs, retrieval-augmented generation, and graph-based reasoning to deliver trusted, explainable lifecycle traceability across requirements, design, test, manufacturing, and operations \cite{kumar_AIEnabledDigital_2023, Jaskie_Agentic_AI_Lifecycle_Traceability_Digital_Thread_2025, Lipizzi_LLM_Augmented_Pipelines_Validated_System_Model_Creation_2025, Helmerich_Transforming_Systems_Engineering_Agentic_AI_2025}. 

Modern systems engineering practice will require the continuous maintenance of a governed artifact network supported by increasingly capable tools \cite{Adeyeye_AI_for_Systems_Engineering_2024}. Understanding this operational context is essential for examining how language models, when introduced into such environments, interact with systems engineering artifacts and where their contributions may succeed or fail. Although recent surveys of U.S. systems engineering curricula suggest that AI coverage is increasingly common, AI is often taught silo-ed rather than being embedded into core, lifecycle workflows \cite{Zhang_AI_Systems_Engineering_Education_Curricula_Training_US_2025}. The following section therefore focuses on the interaction of language models with systems engineering artifacts in digital-thread-enabled workflows.

\subsection{AI Foundations}

\subsubsection{The Field of AI and Where Generative AI Sits}

\acp{llm} fit into the AI landscape as a specific modern approach within a broader stack: \ac{ai} is the umbrella for methods that enable machines to perform tasks associated with intelligence; \ac{ml} is a major subset of \ac{ai} in which systems learn from data rather than relying only on hand-crafted rules; and deep learning is a dominant family of \ac{ml} methods that learn layered representations from large datasets. Within deep learning, \ac{genai} refers to models that can produce new content like text, images, audio, code, and \acp{llm} are generative models specialized in language and often code, typically implemented as neural networks trained to predict and generate sequences of tokens.

    \caption[Where Language Models and Generative AI Falls Within the AI Landscape]{Where Language Models and Generative AI Falls Within the AI Landscape, Adapted from \cite{microsoft_Generativeaiforbeginners_2026}}
    \label{fig:ai-to-genai}
\end{figure}

\acp{llm} are neural networks, typically transformers, trained to predict the next token in a sequence, essentially probabilistic sequence models \cite{vaswani_AttentionAllYou_2017}. They are first pre-trained on massive text corpora to learn general language patterns, then often fine-tuned with human feedback to better follow user intents \cite{commoncrawl_CommonCrawlOpen_2024, ouyang_TrainingLanguageModels_2022}. This two-stage training is the foundation to \ac{llm} generative abilities. Since \acp{llm} generate text by learned statistical associations rather than a grounded world model, they can produce outputs that sound fluent and confident while being incorrect or inconsistent; in other words, an \ac{llm} may "hallucinate" which is defines as generating plausible-sounding but factually false or nonsensical content \cite{kalai_WhyLanguageModelsHallucinate_2025}. Researchers have characterized \acp{llm} as “stochastic parrots,” adept at regurgitating patterns without true understanding \cite{bender_DangersStochasticParrots_2021}. As a result, consistency and groundedness are not guaranteed: an \ac{llm} might contradict itself or deviate from known facts if such outputs have high probability under its training distribution. These issues raise concerns when applying \acp{llm} in domains that require rigor and trustworthiness. 

\subsubsection{Generative AI Foundations}
\label{subsec:genai_foundations}

\ac{genai} refers to \ac{ml} systems that model a data-generating process well enough to produce new samples that resemble the training distribution, rather than only assigning labels or scores to inputs. In practice, this means the model learns a probability distribution over sequences (for text/code) or over other structured domains (e.g., pixels, audio waveforms), enabling it to synthesize novel outputs conditioned on prompts, partial inputs, or other context. \acp{llm} are the language-specialized instantiation of this idea: they represent a conditional generative model over token sequences, producing continuations that are probable under learned patterns \cite{vaswani_AttentionAllYou_2017}.

A distinction for language model engineering contexts is between training and inference. Training establishes the weights that encode statistical regularities in the data, while inference is the deployment-time process of producing outputs from a fixed model given a prompt and context window. During inference, those weights are fixed, and the model generates outputs in response to a prompt within its available context window. A context window is the maximum number of tokens whose content can directly influence the model’s next generated token. It corresponds to the fixed-length input sequence processed by the model’s self-attention mechanism \cite{vaswani_AttentionAllYou_2017}, and bounds how much prior text can affect future predictions \cite{brown_LanguageModelsAre_2020, anthropic_ContextWindows_2025 }.


The model does not retrieve a stored “correct answer” for its response; instead, the model generates text incrementally, selecting the next word (or token) from many plausible options based on probability. This selection process is called decoding. Figure~\ref{fig:llm_prob_tree} illustrates this idea: given a short prompt (“I like …”), the model assigns probabilities to multiple candidate next tokens (e.g., “you,” “being,” “the”), and then continues branching as it generates subsequent tokens. The key point is that generation is inherently choice-based: at each step there is a distribution over alternatives, and the produced output depends on how those alternatives are selected.


\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figs/ch3/llm-probability-tree.png}
    \caption[Language Model Probability Tree]{Language Model Probability Tree, Reproduced from \cite{DemystifyingTemperatureParameterVisual_2024}}
    \label{fig:llm_prob_tree}
\end{figure}



Decoding can be configured to behave more conservatively by favoring the highest-probability continuation or more variably by allowing lower-probability but still plausible alternatives. These settings influence how predictable the output is, and they also influence error behavior. One of the primary settings for setting the probabilities is called temperature.

In probabilistic terms, the model produces a vector of real-valued scores (logits) over the vocabulary, which are converted into a categorical distribution using the softmax function \cite{goodfellow_DeepLearning_2016}. Temperature modifies this distribution at inference time by dividing logits by a scalar \(T\) prior to applying softmax:
\[
P(y_i) = \frac{\exp(z_i/T)}{\sum_j \exp(z_j/T)}.
\]
This operation does not retrain the model or alter its learned knowledge; rather, it reshapes the sampling distribution. Lower temperatures (\(T<1\)) amplify differences among logits, effectively approximating greedy selection and increasing output predictability. Higher temperatures (\(T>1\)) compress these differences, increasing entropy and enabling more diverse continuations \cite{hinton_DistillingKnowledgeNeuralNetwork_2015}. 

Modern \acp{llm} are commonly trained and adapted in multiple stages. Self-supervised pretraining learns broad linguistic competence from large corpora by optimizing next-token prediction objectives \cite{commoncrawl_CommonCrawlOpen_2024}. \ac{sft}, often called instruction tuning when the training data are instruction and response pairs, shaping the model toward task-following behavior \cite{zhang_InstructionTuningLLMSurvey_2025}. Many production systems add preference-based alignment, where the model is optimized against human or proxy preference judgments to increase instruction adherence and reduce harmful behavior \cite{ouyang_TrainingLanguageModels_2022}. While these stages typically improve usability, they also introduce an important measurement implication: the deployed model is now a model whose behavior reflects multiple objective functions and datasets. Empirically, alignment can improve user-rated helpfulness and instruction following while inducing trade-offs or regressions on other capabilities, a phenomenon often described as an alignment tax \cite{ouyang_TrainingLanguageModels_2022, lin_MitigatingAlignmentTaxRLHF_2024}. Accordingly, evaluation should not rely on a single headline metric; holistic suites expose trade-offs across accuracy, robustness, calibration, bias/toxicity, and efficiency \cite{liang_HolisticEvaluationLanguageModels_2023}.

\ac{genai} systems can also be augmented without modifying weights. Prompt engineering and in-context learning condition the model via examples, formats, and constraints embedded in the input \cite{minaee_LargeLanguageModelsSurvey_2025}. \ac{rag} conditions outputs on external references fetched at runtime, combining parametric knowledge in model weights with non-parametric knowledge stored in an index, improving performance on knowledge-intensive tasks and enabling provenance through cited supporting passages \cite{lewis_RetrievalAugmentedGenerationKnowledgeIntensive_2021}. More broadly, retrieval-augmented approaches demonstrate that external context and explicit memory can substantially improve language modeling and downstream task performance without proportionally increasing parameter count \cite{borgeaud_ImprovingLanguageModelsRetrieving_2022}. Tool-using and agentic patterns extend this further by allowing the model to call tool calling mechanisms (e.g. search) and agent skills (e.g. Claude Skills) \cite{anthropic_DonatingModelContext_2025, thelinuxfoundation_LinuxFoundationAnnouncesMCP_2025, bandi_MCPAtlasLargeScaleBenchmarkToolUse_2026, anthropic_ExtendClaudeSkills_2026, barres_t2BenchEvaluatingConversationalAgents_2025}


\subsubsection{Operational Model Characteristics and Deployment Considerations}
\label{subsec:llm_ops_characteristics}

Several operational characteristics affect how \acp{llm} behave in practice and how they can be integrated into engineering environments. Model size is typically reported as the number of parameters, which is a rough proxy for capacity but not a guarantee of domain performance. Context window length determines how much of an artifact network can be provided at once (e.g., a requirement, its rationale, upstream assumptions, interface definitions, and verification evidence), constraining the degree of holistic consistency the model can maintain in a single pass. Recent frontier offerings have pushed context windows into the hundreds of thousands and, in some cases, to approximately one million tokens, which expands what can be presented in a single interaction but does not remove the need for validation and cross-artifact consistency checks \cite{logankilpatrick_Gemini15Pro2M_2024, anthropic_IntroducingSonnet46_2026}.

Deployment constraints motivate quantization, which compresses model weights into lower-precision representations (e.g., 16-bit, 8-bit, 4-bit) to reduce memory footprint and increase throughput, but may also change numerical behavior and, in some settings, affect accuracy or reliability \cite{lang_ComprehensiveStudyQuantization_2024, zhuSurveyModelCompression2024}. 

Finally, it is useful to distinguish between model capability and system or scaffold capability. Many effective \ac{genai} applications rely on the surrounding system: structured prompting, retrieval from authoritative baselines, tool calls, and validation or guardrail steps that check outputs against schemas, constraints, or tests. In the broader \ac{llm} literature, this scaffolding pattern is reflected in tool-augmented and modular architectures where an LLM is paired with external modules or \acp{api} for retrieval, computation, and environment interaction \cite{yao_ReActSynergizingReasoningActing_2023, schick_ToolformerLanguageModelsCan_2023}. 


The broader foundation model ecosystem increasingly targets multimodal perception and spatial intelligence, which are models that can perceive, generate, and reason about 3D environments, motivating future SE evaluation expansion beyond text-centric artifacts \cite{li_WordsWorldsSpatialIntelligence_2025, worldlabs_MarbleMultimodalWorldModel_2025}. However, the scope of this research empirical evaluation is textual tasks and textual work products. Similarly, while \ac{agi} is used as a shorthand for broadly capable, general-purpose AI systems, definitions vary \cite{morris_LevelsAGIOperationalizingProgress_2025, hendrycks_DefinitionAGI_2025}. These concepts inform long-term context and risk discussions, but the present work focuses on near-term, qualification-relevant evidence for text-based \ac{se} artifacts.



\subsection{LLMs as New Actors on the Digital Thread}
\label{subsec:ch2_llm_new_actors_de_thread}

Building on the prior discussion of digital-thread tooling, this subsection focuses specifically on how \acp{llm} reallocate work on the digital thread: systems engineers remain responsible for change governance, but increasingly shift from coordinating primarily human-authored edits to supervising and adjudicating model-generated change proposals across coupled lifecycle artifacts.

Recent literature and practitioner-oriented demonstrations describe a wide range of candidate use cases spanning the \ac{se} lifecycle. Active communities include the \ac{incose} International Symposium and the annual AI4SE \& SE4AI Workshop hosted by the \ac{serc}. \cite{incose_ProceedingsINCOSEInternational_2024, incose_ProceedingsINCOSEInternational_2025, serc_AI4SESE4AIWorkshop_2023, serc_AI4SESE4AIWorkshop_2024, serc_AI4SESE4AIWorkshop_2025}. Practitioner demonstrations include but are not limited to: extracting structured elements from technical text, drafting artifacts such as requirements or interface descriptions, and summarizing and synthesizing across documents.

At the same time, multiple studies emphasize that these capabilities are not yet off-the-shelf substitutes for disciplined engineering practice. For example, \cite{garciaalarcia_BringingSystemsEngineering_2024} argues that while \acp{llm} exhibit strong zero- and few-shot performance on many tasks, direct application to engineering design remains immature without additional structure, motivating the integration of \ac{mbse}-relevant assets, including ontologies, models, and procedures for better context to improve performance and reduce shortcomings such as hallucinations and untraceable behavior \cite{garciaalarcia_BringingSystemsEngineering_2024}. 

Systems engineering-focused AI surveys and case studies accordingly identify opportunities across requirements elicitation and generation, architecture creation and validation, trade-space exploration, automated testing support, fault detection, and operations and maintenance decision support \cite{gadewadikar_SystemsEngineeringLanguageModeling_2024}. More targeted work evaluates \ac{llm} assistance for specific artifact classes. For example, \ac{llm}-assisted requirements generation has been evaluated using criteria grounded directly in \ac{incose} requirements-writing guidance, demonstrating that \ac{llm}-based tools can generate and cluster requirements with quality comparable to human engineers under \ac{incose}-aligned evaluation, while still emphasizing the need for human review \cite{Stein_Leveraging_LLMs_Requirements_Generation_SE_Guidelines_2025}. Such results support the view that \acp{llm} can contribute meaningfully when performance is framed in terms of domain-specific artifact quality.

Recent empirical work demonstrates both the promise and limitations of \ac{llm}-based systems engineering assistance. Controlled experiments on prompt engineering for SE artifact generation reveal that one-shot, few-shot, and chain-of-thought techniques substantially improve quality over zero-shot prompting, though performance varies significantly with temperature and artifact type \cite{SmithCrabb_Jones_Bernard_Prompt_Technique_SE_Artifacts_2024}. When evaluated against \ac{incose} requirements-writing guidelines, \ac{llm}-generated requirements achieve precision of 0.897 and recall of 0.976 for text-based extraction, with quality on par with human SME outputs, though human-in-the-loop validation remains essential \cite{Stein_Leveraging_LLMs_Requirements_Generation_SE_Guidelines_2025}. Fine-tuned models with chain-of-thought prompting significantly outperform baseline approaches on SysMLv2 generation tasks when evaluated using syntax scores, logic scores, MAUVE, and BertScore, demonstrating measurable quality improvements \cite{Wach_LLMs_Accelerate_Complex_Systems_2024}.

A key trend in the digital-thread context is the emergence of agentic toolchains that orchestrate \acp{llm} with engineering repositories, models, and analysis tools to generate connected engineering data products. Agentic platforms in which \acp{llm} are integrated with engineering tools and knowledge graphs to produce traceable outputs across requirements, architecture, simulation, risk, cost, and documentation, reporting substantial reductions in end-to-end workflow time when operating within a unified data environment have been presented \cite{Helmerich_Transforming_Systems_Engineering_Agentic_AI_2025}. Related demonstrations position agentic AI as a bridge between domain experts and formal modeling workflows; for example, bidirectional translation among natural language, images, and SysML v2 models to support iterative validation and collaboration between stakeholders and modelers \cite{Wach_GenGroves_Bridge_Systems_Engineers_Domain_Experts_2025}. Model-centric \ac{mbse} workflows aligned with version control and continuous validation, illustrating how \ac{llm}-driven pipelines can translate technical documentation into validated SysML v2 models using parsers, graph representations, and formal syntax/semantic metrics \cite{Traveller_Agile_Engineering_Agentic_Co_Modelers_2025}.

In mission engineering contexts, \acp{llm} are also being explored as AI-enabled mission engineering workbenches that use retrieval and graph-based techniques to support natural-language interrogation of SysML and simulation models, reducing the cost and time required to obtain actionable insights from complex architectures and analyses \cite{Pennock_AI_Enabled_Mission_Engineering_2025}. In engineering documentation settings, multimodal retrieval is being used to improve the generation and evaluation of system design documents when guided by systems-engineering-specific criteria \cite{Guntupalli_Integrating_Generative_AI_Automation_System_Design_Processes_2024}. In test and evaluation, technical debt management frameworks use \ac{llm}-based document analysis to surface hidden verification, test, and documentation debt early in the V-model lifecycle, framing technical debt as an explicit, engineer-able risk in aerospace contexts \cite{Ouzzif_Leveraging_AI_Manage_Technical_Debt_Test_Evaluation_2025}. Complementing these creation-focused pipelines, other work highlights the operational-use value of \acp{llm} for accessing information embedded in existing \ac{mbse} models through an \ac{llm}-based multi-agent system using a GraphRAG strategy to answer questions over an underlying system model, reducing retrieval effort and improving accessibility for stakeholders who lack deep \ac{mbse} expertise \cite{quast_EnablingHumansAI_2026}.

Agentic AI systems report dramatic productivity gains by orchestrating \acp{llm} with engineering tools across the SE lifecycle. The DaVinci platform reduces system model creation from 100 hours to 20 minutes and generates requirements, architecture, interfaces, behaviors, schedules, and risk matrices in minutes by integrating \acp{llm} with CAD, simulation, and documentation tools \cite{Helmerich_Transforming_Systems_Engineering_Agentic_AI_2025}. Similarly, cognitive-agentic architectures that augment GenAI with verification, simulation, and optimization tools address standalone GenAI's limitations in reliability, explainability, and uncertainty awareness \cite{Lee_CastilloEffen_2024_Rapid_Intelligent_Systems_Engineering, Ahmad_2026_Digital_Twin_Driven_AI_Automation_Optimization}. As these systems shift attention from standalone text generation to tool-using, orchestrated workflows, they also motivate benchmarking approaches that explicitly evaluate agentic \ac{llm} behavior in realistic toolchain settings \cite{mohammadi_EvaluationBenchmarkingLLM_2025}.

However, live red-teaming of persistent, tool-using agents suggests that high-consequence failures often arise in the agentic layer through autonomy with tools, memory, and multi-party communication where minor misunderstandings can cascade into harmful digital-thread actions such as: editing or deleting requirements, mis-applying interface changes across linked models, pushing erroneous commits to configuration baselines, triggering destructive tool runs (e.g., simulations/optimizations with unsafe parameters), leaking controlled artifacts, or propagating spoofed instructions across collaborating agents, highlighting weak “social coherence” as a core gap \cite{shapira_AgentsChaos_2026}. 

Across these studies and demonstrations, the central shift is that \acp{llm} are moving from isolated drafting aids toward integrated digital-thread components that retrieve, transform, and perform functions across the digital thread. Once \ac{llm} outputs inform decisions, modify structured models, or propagate through model-centric workflows, the central issue becomes the degree of delegated authority, the artifact classes affected, and the resulting lifecycle consequences. This motivates a risk framing that links \ac{llm} autonomy to artifact impact, providing a structured lens for interpreting both benefits and failure modes of \ac{llm} adoption in \ac{se}.


\subsection{Spectrum of LLM Risk and Failure Modes in SE}
\label{subsec:ch2_llm_failure_modes}

The same properties that make \acp{llm} attractive for systems engineering, including: fluency, synthesis, and transformation across representations also create distinctive risks when models are used to produce or modify lifecycle artifacts. Many failure modes that are already widely recognized in general-purpose LLM use become more consequential in \ac{se} because they can yield \emph{plausible-but-wrong} artifacts that appear coherent in isolation while degrading traceability, consistency, and auditability across the technical baseline.

To organize these risks in a way that matches realistic engineering adoption, we leverage a two-dimensional LLM risk assessment framework for systems engineering \cite{otten_GenerativeAISystemsRiskAssessment_2026}. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:llm-risk-framework-se}, risk depends jointly on (i) the \emph{autonomy} delegated to the model and (ii) the \emph{impact level} of the artifacts being influenced. The autonomy levels in order of increasing autonomy are: assisted, guided, supervised, fully automated. The impact level of the artifacts in increasing levels are: low, middle, high. The risk classifications are minimal (green), low (yellow), medium (orange), and high risk (red). This framing emphasizes that the relevant issue is not solely that LLMs are probabilistic, but how probabilistic outputs interact with artifact authority and lifecycle consequence.

\paragraph{Minimal Risk: Low-Autonomy, Low-Impact Applications}

 Minimal-risk usages in the surveyed literature is dominated by assistive applications that improve accessibility to existing engineering knowledge and enhance the quality of human-authored artifacts without directly changing baselines. A common pattern is the use of \acp{llm} to structure, normalize, and interrogate engineering information that already exists in requirements documents, standards, or system models. In requirements workflows, this appears as syntax and quality checking—often grounded in established rule sets (e.g., INCOSE-style guidance and template-based criteria) where the \ac{llm} produces proposed changes that remain subject to human acceptance \cite{deoliveira_ImpactAnalysisUsing_2024, Stein_Leveraging_LLMs_Requirements_Generation_SE_Guidelines_2025}. In model-based contexts, minimal-risk use frequently centers on human-friendly interface layers: natural-language Q\&A over SysML models via query translation (e.g. Cypher) and retrieval from knowledge graphs, or graph \ac{rag} style assistants that reduce the effort required to locate and reuse model information \cite{sugawara_ExtractingInformationSystem_2025, quast_EnablingHumansAI_2026, manno_AcceleratingSemanticDigitalThread_2024}; these systems keep the \ac{llm} in a supporting role where outputs are informational or advisory rather than authoritative \cite{dehart_LeveragingLargeLanguage_2024}. At this risk level, the primary lifecycle value is improved throughput and comprehension—faster review, better trace retrieval, reduced friction in stakeholder communication while the baseline remains governed by existing quality management controls.

Within this minimal-risk region, the dominant failure modes are less about catastrophic correctness failures and more about workflow integrity, auditability, and human factors. Several studies explicitly surface limitations consistent with instability: sensitivity to prompt design and decoding settings, reduced accuracy for noisy inputs (e.g., typos and naming inconsistencies), and weaker performance on queries that require multi-hop relational tracing across model elements \cite{SmithCrabb_Jones_Bernard_Prompt_Technique_SE_Artifacts_2024, sugawara_ExtractingInformationSystem_2025}. These behaviors can create inconsistent summaries in documentation workflows and can subtly degrade trust when not reliably grounded in the underlying artifact set. More broadly, position papers within this region caution against automation bias and emphasize that these tools should remain embedded in socio-technical processes with explicit oversight and verification hooks, particularly as organizations scale from single-task assistants toward modular pipelines \cite{dehart_LeveragingLargeLanguage_2024, Raz_SARAI_Architecture_2024, younus_AIOntologyBasedEnhancementsFMEA_2025}. Minimal-risk applications are best interpreted as exploratory, infrastructure information gathering efforts for future higher autonomy deployments: they reveal what breaks repeatability and interpretation while still allowing conventional human review to prevent errors from becoming authoritative changes to the technical baseline.


% ---------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Low Risk: Semi-Autonomous, Limited-Impact Applications}

Low-risk usage reflects a shift from purely supportive tooling toward semi-autonomous assistants that shape engineering reasoning, while still remaining gated by human review before anything becomes part of the controlled baseline. In this region, LLMs and related AI methods are increasingly used to pre-process and interpret messy lifecycle inputs, from unstructured requirements, verification/test artifacts, cybersecurity policies, and systems engineering work products so that engineers can make faster and better-informed decisions. Representative examples include automating portions of requirements preparation for MBSE pipelines \cite{riesener_ApplicationNaturalLanguage_2021}, extracting and prioritizing verification/test/documentation technical debt in test and evaluation artifacts to reduce rework \cite{Ouzzif_Leveraging_AI_Manage_Technical_Debt_Test_Evaluation_2025}, and training AI to employ sets of cybersecurity policies and controls in the context of space system design \cite{orosz_AIAidedDesignDevelopment_2024}. In MBSE toolchains, low-risk studies also place AI inside modeling environments as an “enhancer” that can generate candidate requirements and structural diagrams to accelerate early model creation, while still depending on the engineer to adjudicate correctness and cohesion prior to adoption \cite{johns_AISystemsModeling_2024}. Across these efforts, the recurring value proposition is time savings and improved coverage of review tasks, paired with a consistent design principle: AI augments the SE workflow, but does not replace it \cite{chagnon_CaseStudyAI_2024}.

The characteristic risks at this level are correspondingly subtler than in higher-impact categories: the dominant concern is not direct system hazard, but distortion of engineering judgment through plausible-yet-misaligned outputs, incomplete synthesis, and context-sensitive variability. Case-study evidence shows that effectiveness can depend heavily on prompt context and on sustained dialog rather than “plug-and-play” prompting, making performance difficult to evaluate and increasing the likelihood of inconsistent outcomes across users and sessions \cite{chagnon_CaseStudyAI_2024}. Tool-integrated generation can also introduce structural quality defects such as redundancy, weak cohesion, or gaps in model consistency that are easy to miss during superficial review, especially when the AI output is fluent and voluminous \cite{johns_AISystemsModeling_2024}. Similarly, debt-identification and document-analysis systems must contend with false positives, auditability, and program-to-program generalization, which motivates continuous monitoring and explicit validation workflows even when the consequences remain bounded \cite{Ouzzif_Leveraging_AI_Manage_Technical_Debt_Test_Evaluation_2025}. For these reasons, low-risk adoption typically calls for governance mechanisms that keep outputs transparent and reviewable (e.g., traceable rationales, validation checkpoints, and testable risk/ethics requirements integrated into SE methods and assessments) so that AI assistance strengthens decision-making without quietly becoming an unqualified source of authority \cite{heckle2024responsible, Farrington_Reiner_Responsible_Use_AI_Sustainability_2024}.


% ---------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{Medium Risk: Decision-Influencing Applications }

Medium-risk usage arises when \textbackslash{}acp\{llm\}-enabled pipelines begin to influence engineering decisions and produce artifacts that can ripple through a digital thread, despite the presence of human review. The literature in this region increasingly moves beyond isolated assistance toward integrated digital engineering workflows: text-to-model and multimodal-to-model transformations, model querying and mutation mediated by tool chains, and agentic orchestration across requirements, architecture, simulation, documentation, and project governance \cite{Bradshaw_Accelerating_Systems_Engineering_Generative_AI_2025, Helmerich_Transforming_Systems_Engineering_Agentic_AI_2025, Wach_LLMs_Accelerate_Complex_Systems_2024, vangundy_DevelopingConceptsOperationsUsing_2024, naveau_LLMCopilotsDomainSpecific_2024}. In these settings, the central risk is that an error becomes structural: mis-specified interfaces, incomplete functional allocations, incorrect diagram semantics, or untraceable policy interpretations that then influence subsequent modeling, analysis, and verification activities. 


Many medium-risk contributions focus on engineering trustworthy translation rather than unconstrained generation, by introducing structured representations and validation gates that reduce the degrees of freedom available to the model. LLM-augmented modeling pipelines increasingly combine extraction into knowledge graphs, semantic alignment, and \ac{sme} validation as explicit assurance mechanisms, enabling retrieval and reasoning over a curated and corrected representation of system knowledge \cite{Lipizzi_LLM_Augmented_Pipelines_Validated_System_Model_Creation_2025, garciaalarcia_BringingSystemsEngineering_2024}. However, the evaluation rigor remains mixed: some systems report qualitative scoring and \ac{llm}-as-judge assessments without sufficient disclosure of prompts, grader configuration, or reproducibility controls, limiting their utility as qualification evidence for adoption \cite{Collard_Automated_Knowledge_Base_Generation_Open_Source_LLMs_RAG_2025}. Practitioner-oriented syntheses reinforce this point by emphasizing that as AI begins to influence decisions and artifacts, the limiting factor becomes assurance through governance, configuration control, validation metrics, and accountability \cite{Lee_CastilloEffen_2024_Rapid_Intelligent_Systems_Engineering, sheard_AIOverviewCaveats_2025}. Collectively, these studies suggest that the medium-risk region is where \acp{llm} transition from decision-support to decision-steering components of the digital thread. The key research and practice gap is the development of repeatable evaluation protocols, artifact-level validity metrics, and assurance arguments that justify when and how AI-generated artifacts may be admitted into a controlled engineering baseline.


% ---------------------------------------------------------
\paragraph{High Risk: Safety-Critical and Configuration-Influencing Applications }

High-risk usage encompasses deployments where \acp{llm} operate with high delegated autonomy and their outputs directly influence safety- or mission-critical artifacts, including configurations, control logic, and verification evidence. In this region, failures can translate into system-level hazard, assurance violations, or irreversible lifecycle commitments because the model is actively participating in, or executing, decisions that determine what the system will do and how that behavior will be justified. Vision papers on \ac{ai} and \ac{mbse} integration emphasize that, despite rapid progress in AI capabilities, the field lacks a coherent, systematic foundation for operationalizing \ac{ai} within \ac{mbse} at the authority levels associated with high-impact engineering decisions, motivating continued research on responsible integration \cite{chami_ArtificialIntelligenceCapabilities_2022}. 
At the same time, recent automation platforms illustrate the direction of travel: systems that automate requirements derivation, rewrite and quality-check artifact sets, generate candidate architectures, and produce traceability links at scale can yield dramatic throughput gains and thereby increase the temptation to shift oversight from human-in-the-loop review toward human-on-the-loop supervision, or even human-out-of-the-loop acceptance of AI-produced artifacts \cite{cannizzaro_SystemsEngineeringAutomation_2025}.

High-risk deployment is best characterized as an assurance and governance challenge rather than a pure capability challenge. The autonomy versus impact framework used in this chapter makes this explicit by treating risk as a native property of the integration context \cite{otten_GenerativeAISystemsRiskAssessment_2026}. In this regime, well-known \ac{llm} challenges including hallucination and prompt brittleness are unacceptable unless mitigated through observability, auditability, safeguards, and fail-safe fallback mechanisms that preserve human authority \cite{kalai_WhyLanguageModelsHallucinate_2025,otten_GenerativeAISystemsRiskAssessment_2026, sclar_QuantifyingLanguageModelsSensitivity_2024}. Agentic digital thread demonstrations underscore both the opportunity and the hazard: by collapsing end-to-end workflows across requirements, architecture, simulation, risk, and documentation, such platforms increase the pace at which errors can be introduced and propagated, placing additional burden on governance, accountability, and review capacity \cite{Helmerich_Transforming_Systems_Engineering_Agentic_AI_2025}. The practical implication is that scaling into high-autonomy, high-impact regions will require standardized qualification evidence and safety-case-like assurance arguments such as maturity metrics for robustness and measurable limits on autonomy-risk exposure before AI-generated artifacts can be responsibly admitted into mission- or safety-critical authoritative lifecycle artifacts \cite{otten_GenerativeAISystemsRiskAssessment_2026, NIST_ArtificialIntelligenceRisk_2023, Johnson_Operationalizing_AI_Assurance_2025, Pomales_Measuring_Influencing_Trustworthiness_AI_Enabled_Systems_2025}.

\paragraph{Mapping SE Literature to Autonomy-Impact Risk Matrix}

The autonomy–impact matrix in Table \ref{tab:llm_autonomy_impact_matrix_se} synthesizes the surveyed literature on AI-assisted \ac{se} and maps it to a \ac{llm} risk framework for \ac{se}. Although the original formulation was developed in an automotive context, the framework has been abstracted here to apply across the \ac{se} domain as a whole, preserving its autonomy–impact logic. The table demonstrates how published work already spans multiple regions of the matrix. The majority of current applications concentrate in low-impact and assisted or guided autonomy configurations, reflecting cautious integration patterns focused on documentation, retrieval, and analytical support. Emerging research increasingly explores supervised execution in middle-impact contexts, particularly in architecture generation, requirements derivation, and model transformation workflows. Fully autonomous research in high-impact regions remains sparse and is primarily discussed in terms of governance, assurance, and responsible deployment rather than empirical fielded capability. This distribution suggests that the field is progressing from augmentation toward higher-authority integration, while formal assurance mechanisms lag behind increases in delegated autonomy. The framework therefore provides both a descriptive taxonomy of current practice and a prescriptive lens for evaluating future deployments. Bibliometric mapping of the \ac{mbse} literature further supports this interpretation: AI- and LLM-related keywords remain partially separated from core systems engineering and SysML clusters, indicating that integration between generative AI techniques and established model-based practices is still emerging rather than fully consolidated \cite{muthukumar_MappingLandscapeModelbased_2026}. This structural separation reinforces the need for systematic qualification evidence and assurance strategies before higher-autonomy deployments become embedded within safety- or mission-critical engineering workflows.

\label{sec:benchmarking_as_qualification_evidence}

Benchmarking is often treated as a way to rank models, but in domain practice it functions as \emph{qualification evidence}: evidence supporting claims about whether a model is suitable for an intended use with known consequences of failure. This section develops that evidence-oriented view and applies it to \ac{se}, where errors can propagate across lifecycle decisions and assurance artifacts. The next section analyzes sources of evaluation unreliability, followed by a comparison of evaluation modalities as distinct evidence types and a discussion of cost and computational constraints.


\subsection{Benchmarking in the LLM ecosystem}
\label{subsec:benchmarking_llm_ecosystem}

Large language models (LLMs) are transformer-based neural networks trained to predict and generate sequences of text, an approach popularized by the attention-based architecture that enabled scaling to large corpora and model sizes \cite{vaswani_AttentionAllYou_2017}. In contemporary machine learning practice, LLM benchmarking typically refers to evaluating models on standardized datasets and tasks under a defined protocol to (i) compare models, (ii) track progress over time, and (iii) provide selection signals for downstream use \cite{huggingfaceOpenLLMLeaderboard2024, myrzakhan_OpenLLMLeaderboard_2024}. Benchmarking is therefore widely treated as a mechanism for establishing shared reference points across rapidly evolving model families.

A unified leaderboard is often an attractive ideal because it provides a stable, interpretable comparison signal across models. Yet the field’s fast-moving and diversifying benchmark ecosystem makes it challenging for any single leaderboard to remain comprehensive, current, and representative of the range of intended uses. Recent surveys catalog broad families of benchmarks and protocols including general capability suites, reasoning-focused evaluations, factuality and hallucination stress tests, safety and alignment evaluations, and domain benchmarks tailored to specialized work \cite{chang_SurveyEvaluationLarge_2024, ni_SurveyLargeLanguageBenchmarks_2025}. This proliferation reflects a practical realization: different benchmark designs capture different aspects of performance, so no single score can serve as a universal proxy for "model quality" across all intended uses.

Figure~\ref{fig:timeline_of_benchmarks} summarizes the contemporary benchmarking landscape as three broad families of measurement instruments: \emph{domain-specific benchmarks} (left), \emph{general capability benchmarks} (center), and \emph{target-specific benchmarks} (right). This organization is useful for evidence-oriented interpretation because each family tends to support different classes of claims about model behavior \cite{ni_SurveyLargeLanguageBenchmarks_2025}.

The center band of Figure~\ref{fig:timeline_of_benchmarks} represents general capability benchmarks designed to provide broadly comparable, cross-model signals of overall competence, enabling tracking of progress over time and providing coarse selection signals. Surveys of LLM evaluation commonly treat this family as the "reference layer" of evaluation practice because it offers stable measurement protocols and community-recognized comparison points \cite{chang_SurveyEvaluationLarge_2024, ni_SurveyLargeLanguageBenchmarks_2025}. 

The left band of Figure~\ref{fig:timeline_of_benchmarks} highlights the acceleration of domain-specific benchmarking as \acp{llm} continue to move toward specialty professional settings where definitions of correctness, acceptable uncertainty, and workflow constraints differ materially from general  practice. This includes but is not limited to domain benchmarks in biomedicine and clinical reasoning \cite{jin_PubMedQADatasetBiomedicalResearch_2019, Jin_Li_2024_Benchmarking_LLMs_EBM}, engineering and technical domains \cite{dorisDesignQAMultimodalBenchmark2024, syedBenchmarkingCapabilitiesLarge2024,
li_EEEBenchComprehensiveMultimodalElectrical_2025, zhouEngiBenchBenchmarkEvaluating2025, mudurFEABenchEvaluatingLanguage2025}, law \cite{fei_LawBenchBenchmarkingLegal_2023, guha_LegalBenchCollaborativelyBuilt_2023, chalkidis_LexGLUEBenchmarkDataset_2022}, and finance \cite{islam_FinanceBenchNewBenchmarkFinancial_2023}. Evidence across these efforts suggests that strong performance on broad or generic evaluation does not guarantee robust performance when domain terminology, domain rules, and domain workflows are central to correctness \cite{anjum_DomainSpecificBenchmarks_2025}.

The right band of Figure~\ref{fig:timeline_of_benchmarks} reflects benchmarks that focus on particular evaluation targets or failure modes rather than overall capability, often providing more directly interpretable evidence for a specific risk dimension. This includes benchmarks emphasizing multi-step reasoning and evidence integration (e.g., multi-hop question and answering requiring the model to combine information across sources) \cite{yang_HotpotQADatasetDiverse_2018}, reasoning about actions and change under controlled linguistic confounds \cite{Wei_G_He_TRAC_A_Textual_Benchmark_for_Reasoning_2022}, and factuality-oriented or hallucination-oriented evaluations that explicitly stress-test truthfulness and verification \cite{li_HaluEvalLargeScaleHallucination_2023, gupta_DialFactBenchmarkFactChecking_2022}.  A closely related subset is execution-grounded evaluation, where correctness depends on traceable interaction with tools, code, solvers, or algorithmic traces rather than purely on fluent text. Across domains, this matters because plausible reasoning can fail under execution: FEABench shows brittleness in simulation-intensive workflows due to subtle physics or solver misconfigurations and execution-grounded automated research environments likewise demonstrate that generating plausible ideas is insufficient without executable verification and feedback \cite{mudurFEABenchEvaluatingLanguage2025, Markeeva_CLRS_reasoning_benchmark_2024, si_ExecutionGroundedAutomatedAIResearch_2026}.

\subsection{Benchmarking as Qualification Evidence in Systems Engineering}
\label{subsec:benchmarking_as_qualification_evidence_se}

This dissertation treats qualification as having sufficient support to make bounded claims that a capability is fit for a specified intended use, under defined constraints and consequence expectations. While SE does not define qualification as a formal construct for model assessment, the framing mirrors the logic of \ac{vandv}: confidence is built by connecting claims to lifecycle work products, verifying required properties, and validating acceptable behavior under representative conditions and uncertainties \cite{standard_ieee_15288_2023, walden_systems_engineering_handbook_2015}.

Benchmarking becomes qualification-relevant in SE because \ac{llm} outputs are rarely consumed in isolation. Instead, they are introduced into lifecycle workflows and technical baselines that include requirements, interfaces, design descriptions, trade studies, verification artifacts, rationale, and risk management products. In digital engineering and \ac{mbse} environments, these work products are increasingly linked through a digital thread, and their internal consistency and traceability are part of what makes the baseline auditable and governable \cite{deputyassistantsecretaryofdefense_DepartmentDefenseDigitalStrategy_2018, estefanSurveyModelBasedSystems2008}. Errors introduced upstream can therefore propagate downstream: an unclear or incorrect requirement can bias architecture decisions and verification planning; an omitted constraint can remain latent until integration; a mischaracterized risk can distort mitigation priorities. The consequence profile is not occasional wrong text but lifecycle drift that can become embedded in authoritative artifacts.

SE-facing risk frameworks sharpen this point by explicitly tying assurance expectations to how an LLM is used in the workflow and how consequential its outputs are. For example, the LLM Risk Assessment Framework classifies LLM-enabled applications along autonomy and impact dimensions to determine corresponding risk levels and, by implication, appropriate validation strategies and human oversight requirements \cite{otten_GenerativeAISystemsRiskAssessment_2026}. In evidence terms, this means the same underlying model can require materially different qualification evidence depending on whether it is used as a supportive assistant for low-impact drafting versus an automated actor influencing high-impact engineering decisions.

This shifts what benchmark evidence must do. The most consequential failures are often those that (i) appear plausible at the point of creation, (ii) evade detection during routine review, and (iii) influence downstream engineering decisions. In SE terms, these are latent and propagative defects. They may not present as obviously incorrect prose; they may present as missing assumptions, incomplete updates across lifecycle products, or content that is locally coherent but globally misaligned with the evolving baseline. This is why qualification-oriented benchmarking emphasizes not only average correctness but also the ability of an evaluation to expose the failure modes that matter for the intended use and consequence class \cite{amodei_ConcreteProblemsAISafety_2016}.

Within this dissertation, the evidence-oriented view of benchmarking is formalized through a \ac{se} specific benchmark, SysEngBench \cite{bell_IntroducingSysEngBenchNovel_2024, bell_RabellSysEngBenchDatasets_2024, Bell2025SysEngBench}.  More broadly, evaluations that inform SE adoption decisions should be interpreted as contextual evidence, with an explicit rationale for why the measured behavior is relevant to the intended workflow and consequences of failure, rather than assuming transfer from generic benchmarks. 

Related work in adjacent defense and engineering contexts highlights both the need for SE-relevant evidence and the risks of under-specified evaluation designs. For example, work applying \ac{llm} to automated knowledge-base generation over digital engineering guidance used LLM-based grading across multiple qualitative dimensions, while also showing limited disclosure of evaluation settings and prompts can reduce repeatability and weaken the strength of the resulting qualification evidence \cite{Collard_Automated_Knowledge_Base_Generation_Open_Source_LLMs_RAG_2025}. Defense benchmarks targeting operational knowledge \cite{parham_JointStaffBench_2025} and reliability-focused benchmarks emphasizing misuse modes and robustness \cite{hu_UseMisusePreTrained_2024} both reinforce the need to ground evaluation in intended use and consequence-relevant failure modes rather than generic capability extrapolation. 


\subsection{Governance as the Forcing Function}
\label{subsec:governance_forcing_function}

 
Governance frameworks increasingly require documented, risk-tiered evaluation evidence for \ac{ai} uses, making benchmarking hard to treat as optional in practice. In federal and defense contexts, risk management and policy guidance increasingly require that AI systems be governed according to intended use, consequence, and lifecycle risk. The \ac{nist} AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) explicitly frames AI risk as socio-technical and lifecycle-spanning, emphasizing governance, contextual mapping, measurement, and ongoing management rather than one-time validation \cite{NIST_ArtificialIntelligenceRisk_2023}. Policy instruments similarly push agencies toward inventorying AI uses, applying risk-based safeguards, and documenting evidence commensurate with impacts \cite{Office_of_Management_and_Budget_2024_M24-10_AI_Governance, Department_of_Homeland_Security_2023_AI_Acquisition_Use}.

SE-facing assurance efforts extend this logic into practice: emphasizing lifecycle-aligned assurance activities, repeatable test and evaluation strategies, and reusable assurance artifacts \cite{Johnson_Operationalizing_AI_Assurance_2025, Lanus_Tailorable_Risk_Informed_AI_Test_Evaluation_Strategy_2025, Charnetzki_Hazard_Analysis_RAG_LLM_Systems_2025}. The same AI capability can present different risk–performance tradeoffs depending on its workflow integration, motivating evaluation techniques tailored to the domain-specific workflow \cite{Szajnfarber_SERC_Perspective_AI4SE_SE4AI_2025, heckle2024responsible}. Collectively, these governance and assurance perspectives reinforce a central implication for this dissertation: benchmark scores can contribute to an assurance argument, but only when treated as bounded, contextual evidence aligned to intended use, failure consequences, and lifecycle controls.

Even when benchmarking is framed correctly as qualification evidence, the evidence can be distorted if the evaluation itself is unreliable, a concern that motivates calls for benchmarks and test regimes that are explicitly aligned to engineering use cases and assurance needs \cite{raz_ExplainableAICounterfactuals_2023}. In practice, results can vary due to dataset lifecycle hazards such as leakage, contamination, protocol sensitivity, and reproducibility limits; these effects can produce measurements that appear stable while masking uncertainty about what a benchmark actually captured 
\cite{Cheng_2024_Benchmark_Data_Contamination, magar_DataContaminationMemorizationExploitation_2022, weber_MindInstructionsHolisticEvaluation_2023}.

Benchmarking evidence can misrepresent intended-use readiness when the measurement construct is ambiguous or when the evaluation protocol is unstable. A benchmark score only carries qualification weight if the measurement process is interpretable, reproducible, and aligned with the lifecycle decisions it is meant to inform. This section therefore treats \ac{llm} evaluation as a measurement problem: what is being measured, how stably it is measured, and under what conditions a reported score can support bounded claims about suitability for intended use.

Threats to evaluation reliability have been organized into four cross-cutting categories that operate across common modalities (e.g., multiple-choice, open-form generation, and judge-based scoring): (i) structural properties of the task format; (ii) benchmark and dataset lifecycle hazards; (iii) protocol and tooling sensitivity; and (iv) scoring instability and evaluator bias. In \ac{se}, these threats are can weakening traceability, consistency, and auditability throughout the digital thread.

\subsection{Structural Threats to Validity}
\label{subsec:ch2_structural_threats}

Structural threats arise from the format of the evaluation itself. In measurement terms, modalities function as instruments: they determine which capabilities are observable, how tightly responses are constrained, and what failure modes are likely to be hidden. In artifact-centric domains such as systems engineering, it is therefore plausible that no single modality provides sufficient evidence on its own: bounded formats can scale but may under-measure, while open formats broaden construct coverage but shift the burden onto grading reliability.

\acp{mcq} formats provide bounded outputs and objective scoring, but introduce format-induced vulnerabilities. Empirical studies demonstrate option position bias and token-identifier bias, where models assign disproportionate probability to particular option choices(e.g., \emph{A}/\emph{B}/\emph{C}/\emph{D}) independent of content \cite{zheng_LargeLanguageModelsAre_2024}. \acp{mcq} also admit elimination shortcuts and surface-regularity exploitation: a model can appear correct as long as the true answer is present among the options, yet fail when the option set is perturbed (e.g., by replacing a correct option with "none of the above") \cite{wang_AnswersReviewingRationality_2024}. 

Distractor construction is a particularly brittle degree of freedom. Poorly constructed distractors can inflate scores by making the correct answer easy to identify through elimination, while adversarially constructed distractors can reveal failure modes invisible under benign options \cite{ghanem_DISTO_2023, yigitAdversarialDistractorGeneration2025}. This makes robustness to option construction a measurement property. For defensible evidence, evaluation should assess sensitivity to option order, option identifiers, and distractor quality. More broadly, benchmark designers have emphasized that constructing challenging \acp{mcq} is difficult precisely because strong test-takers (human or model) can exploit elimination strategies unless distractors are comparably hard to dismiss \cite{TidorVlad_Pricope_2025_HardML_Benchmark}.

\acp{mcq} can be appropriate when the goal is to choose among explicitly provided alternatives, but the format can oversimplify complex topics and obscure whether a model could independently produce, justify, or maintain traceability for those alternatives. Systems engineering scenarios that require deep reasoning, synthesis across concepts, or subtle trade-offs are often poorly captured by a small set of fixed options, so MCQ performance may not reflect genuine understanding or explainable reasoning \cite{zheng_LargeLanguageModelsAre_2024, fu_mcq_reasoning_more_confident_when_wrong_2025}. Paired evaluations in safety and value-alignment settings further show that MCQ-style judgments can reveal vulnerabilities that do not appear in open-ended responses, reinforcing that MCQ and generative modalities are not interchangeable evidence signals \cite{wang_fake_alignment_2024}. Taken together, MCQ accuracy alone does not characterize reliability, since it can be sensitive to option ordering, distractor construction, and other format constraints. In some cases, deliberately including no correct option can also help probe reasoning and critical thinking rather than recognition under constraints \cite{goral_when_all_options_are_wrong_2024}.

\acp{osq} remove pre-defined, constrained answer sets and better approximate synthesis-oriented work products. They can expose reasoning structure, assumptions, omissions, and the organization of technical content—properties that matter for traceability and auditability. However, open-form tasks introduce interpretive variability: multiple responses may be acceptable under different assumptions, and reasoning chains may differ in structure while achieving similar conclusions. This reliability concerns shift from selection bias toward scoring stability and evaluator behavior has long been visible in text-generation evaluation: overlap-based metrics such as BLEU enabled scalable scoring, but they also illustrated how proxy measures can diverge from human judgments of adequacy and meaning \cite{papineniBleuMethodAutomatic2002}.

Open formats also make it easier for models to succeed rhetorically while failing technically. Textual responses can be fluent and plausible yet shallow or weakly grounded. This is especially problematic in engineering contexts where correctness depends on explicit assumptions and constraints. Empirical work shows that models can express high confidence even when reasoning is wrong, motivating evaluation designs that test faithful reasoning rather than persuasive presentation \cite{fu_mcq_reasoning_more_confident_when_wrong_2025, Sourav_Banerjee_2024_Vulnerability_LLM_Benchmarks}.

\subsection{Dataset Lifecycle Threats}
\label{subsec:ch2_dataset_threats}

Benchmarks evolve over time, and lifecycle hazards can systematically distort measured performance.

Benchmark creation choices determine what a benchmark can measure. Construction methods include expert authoring, crowdsourcing, web mining, gamification, automated synthesis, and adaptation from existing resources; these are often combined \cite{davisBenchmarksAutomatedCommonsense_2023}. 
Each choice shapes question quality, difficulty calibration, and the benchmark’s true sensitivity. For example, item defects such as incorrect labels, multiple correct answers, and ambiguous wording, along with unnatural language, can confound evaluation and invite shortcut learning \cite{davisBenchmarksAutomatedCommonsense_2023}. Difficulty design also matters: Bloom-style mappings and cognitive complexity frameworks can help align item difficulty with intended constructs \cite{blooms_taxonomy_1956, Liangtai_Sun_2023_SciEval_Benchmark, R_Sujay_2024_Question_Complexity_Estimation}.

Contamination and leakage further distort measured performance when benchmark content is exposed during pre-training, fine-tuning, or instruction tuning \cite{Cheng_2024_Benchmark_Data_Contamination, oren_black_box_contamination_2023}. Contamination is not discrete; semantic exposure, metadata visibility, language translations, and public discussion can also bias results. Recent work distinguishes contamination types and proposes detection approaches beyond naive string matching \cite{Cheng_2024_Benchmark_Data_Contamination, oren_black_box_contamination_2023, yangRethinkingBenchmarkContamination2023, sainzNLPEvaluationTrouble2023, Ahuja_2024_Multilingual_Benchmark_Contamination}. Statistical perspectives broaden contamination to include any inflated, non-generalizing performance and attempt to quantify effects using reference benchmarks and model sets \cite{Dekoninck_2024_ConStat}. Additional analyses argue that contamination effects may attenuate as training data grows through forgetting dynamics, complicating causal attributions from behavior to specific training exposures \cite{Bordt_2024_Forgetting_Data_Contamination}. 

As models approach ceiling performance, saturation reduces a benchmark's discriminative power and can mask robustness differences. Once most models achieve near-ceiling performance, the benchmark can conceal important distinctions such as sensitivity to perturbations, reasoning faithfulness, or brittleness to distribution shift. Emerging work argues for explicit criteria and processes to deprecate benchmarks that no longer serve their purpose \cite{Ayrton_San_Joaquin_2025_Deprecating_Benchmarks}.

Synthetic and semi-synthetic generation further scales training data and benchmark construction, but introduces new sensitivity parameters. Results can vary with generation seeds, prompt templates, filtering heuristics, and model choice \cite{chen_diversity_2024, varshneyCreatingSyntheticData2024}. Synthetic data is now widely used for both training and benchmark creation, and recent survey work organizes the landscape around common workflow stages such as generation, filtering, diversification, and validation while identifying open gaps in methodology and evaluation \cite{long_LLMsDrivenSyntheticData_2024}. Modern pipelines typically generate candidate items, filter for quality, create variants through paraphrasing and difficulty escalation, and incorporate some degree of human review \cite{gunasekar_Textbooks_Are_All_2023, xu_Wizard_LMEmpoweringLarge2025, fuQGEvalBenchmarkingMultidimensional2024}. Variation in prompts, seeds, model family, and real-to-synthetic mixing ratios can measurably shift downstream performance and robustness, motivating explicit methods to assess synthetic data quality and diversity \cite{chenDiversitySyntheticData2024}. In practice, synthetic datasets are now common across many domains and tasks \cite{thakurLeveragingLLMsSynthesizing2023, cifarelliSafuraiCsharpHarnessingSynthetic2023, huggingface_syntheticdatamoney_2024}.

\subsection{Protocol and Tooling Threats}
\label{subsec:ch2_protocol_threats}

Even when datasets are stable, evaluation outcomes can be highly sensitive to implementation details. For qualification-oriented evidence, this sensitivity is itself a measurement property and determines whether a reported score is reproducible and interpretable under the conditions of use.

Selective reporting of prompt variants, harness configurations, or evaluation subsets can inflate claims. Evaluation runners also encode many silent assumptions such as answer extraction, normalization, stop sequences, and dataset version pinning. Small differences in these settings can produce nontrivial score deltas \cite{liang_HolisticEvaluationLanguage_2023, laskarSystematicSurveyCritical2024}. Critiques of benchmarking practice argue that this freedom in implementation choices is a major contributor to non-reproducible results and non-comparable claims \cite{mcintoshInadequaciesLargeLanguage2024}. 

Protocol sensitivity is often most visible in prompt design. Few-shot examples, instruction framing, stop sequences, and answer extraction logic can shift scores materially. This affects measurement stability and can also undermine construct validity as the prompt template can inadvertently change what capability is being tested \cite{gao_FrameworkFewshotLanguage_2024}. Engineering-focused benchmark work makes this explicit by using multiple prompt variants to elicit different required capabilities, showing that prompt form is frequently inseparable from the construct under evaluation \cite{zhouEngiBenchBenchmarkEvaluating2025}. 

Even when prompts are fixed, inference settings introduce additional variance. Temperature, sampling strategy, and nondeterminism in the inference stack can change outcomes across repeated runs. Qualification-oriented evaluation therefore often constrains decoding by setting temperature to zero to reduce stochastic effects \cite{gao_LanguageModelEvaluation_2024, gao_FrameworkFewshotLanguage_2024}.

The design implication is to treat benchmarking as a controlled experiment. For benchmarking evidence to function as qualification evidence, protocols must be fully specified and auditable. This includes dataset version, prompt template, decoding parameters, judge model and rubric. When sensitivity is high, repeated trials, perturbation tests, and uncertainty estimates provide more defensible evidence than single point estimates.

\subsection{Judge Scoring and Evaluator Threats}
\label{subsec:ch2_scoring_threats}



 %%%%%% CITE SOME ADDITIONAL META EVALUATION STUDIES HERE.
Scoring introduces a second measurement instrument layered onto model outputs. This is most visible for open-form tasks, where correctness is not reducible to exact matching and evaluators must formalize quality through rubrics, scales, or comparative judgments. As a result, evaluation reliability depends not only on the model being tested, but also on the properties and stability of the scoring instrument.

A first failure mode is metric misalignment. Classic generation metrics such as BLEU show how scalable proxies can diverge from human judgment by rewarding surface overlap rather than semantic adequacy \cite{papineniBleuMethodAutomatic2002}. Modern \ac{llm} evaluation inherits analogous risks when rubric proxies or heuristic metrics replace expert review.

Human grading improves construct fidelity, but it introduces its own variance. Even with detailed rubrics, graders can disagree about how to weight assumptions, intermediate reasoning, and presentation quality. This disagreement is especially salient in technical domains where multiple solution paths can be defensible, creating inter-rater variability alongside training and scalability constraints.

LLM-based grading offers scale, but introduces distinct validity risks. Recent surveys formalize the LLM-as-a-judge paradigm and argue that reliability requires careful design, standardization, and meta-evaluation \cite{gu_survey_on_llm_as_a_judge_2025, li_llms_as_judges_2024}. Empirical studies show that even strong judge models can fall short of inter-human agreement and that judge scores can vary materially with prompt design, task domain, and evaluation setup \cite{thakurJudgingJudgesEvaluating2025, huang_EmpiricalStudyLLMasaJudgeLLM_2025}. Other work proposes trained judges and development pipelines that include human-labeled meta-evaluation benchmarks, while cautioning that naive scaling or distillation does not guarantee judge quality improvements \cite{huTrainingLLMasaJudgeModel2025}.

Several recurring judge biases have been documented. Pairwise judges can exhibit position bias \cite{zheng_JudgingLLMasaJudgeMTBench_2023}. Judges can favor longer outputs through verbosity or length bias even when quality is unchanged \cite{zheng_JudgingLLMasaJudgeMTBench_2023, dubois_Length_Controlled_Alpaca_Eval_Simple_2025}. They can also display self-enhancement bias by preferring responses that resemble the judge model \cite{zheng_JudgingLLMasaJudgeMTBench_2023}. Scores can be sensitive to rubric phrasing and evaluation instructions \cite{zhouMitigatingBiasLarge2024}. Finally, judges may be unreliable on math- or reasoning-heavy questions when the judge cannot consistently determine the correct solution \cite{zheng_JudgingLLMasaJudgeMTBench_2023}.

These effects can distort rankings and undermine comparability across studies and across judge model versions. Scoring scales and rating rules also introduce degrees of freedom. Some benchmarks use discrete ordinal scales such as 1 to 5 that compress variation and amplify threshold effects, while others use continuous or comparative schemes that shift sensitivity and variance properties \cite{li_WhenLLMsMeetCunning_2024, zheng_JudgingLLMasaJudgeMTBench_2023}. Length-controlled evaluation variants further show that length bias can be large enough to change leaderboard orderings, motivating explicit controls or causal adjustments when comparing systems \cite{dubois_Length_Controlled_Alpaca_Eval_Simple_2025}.

Mitigation strategies include position swapping, length normalization and length-controlled scoring, calibration sets, multi-judge aggregation, and uncertainty reporting through confidence intervals and stability checks across prompts and judge seeds \cite{Tianle_Li_2024_BenchBuilder_ArenaHard_LLM_Benchmarking, zhouMitigatingBiasLarge2024}. Without explicit calibration and meta-evaluation, judge-based scoring can become circular, with a model evaluating other models under correlated training priors and producing scores that appear stable while remaining misaligned with stakeholder-relevant notions of quality.

The design implication is that scorer validity must be argued rather than assumed. For qualification evidence, scoring validity is part of the claim. If the evaluator is unstable, biased, or weakly aligned to domain-expert judgment, then high benchmark scores provide limited support for intended-use readiness even when the underlying task format is well designed.

Qualification evidence is not free: evidence depth and reliability depend on the cost of running models, generating responses, and, in \ac{osq} settings, judging outputs. These costs impose feasibility constraints on evaluation pipelines, shaping practical choices about sample size, replication, modality mix, response-length policy, and scoring strategy. In this dissertation, cost is treated primarily as a trade-space variable and reporting dimension that bounds what can be evaluated at scale, rather than as a direct pass/fail criterion for qualification decisions.


\subsection{Cost as a Feasibility Constraint on Qualification Evidence}
\label{subsec:ch2_cost_feasibility}

Evaluation for intended use is often discussed in terms of validity and reliability, such as whether an instrument measures what it claims to measure and whether it does so consistently. Evaluation is also constrained by feasibility: compute access, wall clock time, and usage-based pricing determine how much evidence can be collected and how robustly it can be tested. These constraints are essential for \acp{llm} because costs scale with input context length, output length, and the number of repeated runs required for robustness such as prompt variants \cite{li_llms_as_judges_2024, lu2024bias}. For \acp{osq}, feasibility is further shaped by scoring overhead: the cost of human grading or the additional inference required for LLM-as-a-judge pipelines can dominate the overall evaluation budget \cite{huang_LLMasaJudgeScalableTest_2025, tang_LearningEfficientMultiTurn_2026}. As a result, evaluation becomes a practical trade-space problem in which breadth (more items, more topics) competes with depth (richer responses, artifact-proximal tasks) and reliability (replication and stability checks) competes with throughput.

Cost trade-space reasoning is a familiar theme in \ac{se}. Parametric models such as \ac{cocomo} estimate software development cost using drivers related to size, complexity, and personnel factors \cite{boehm1981software_engineering_economics, boehm_COCOMO2.0_1995}. Analogously, \ac{cosysmo} extends parametric estimation to systems engineering effort by accounting for system complexity, integration, and related drivers \cite{valerdi_COSYSMO_2010, wang_ReuseSystemsEngineering_2010, alstadDevelopmentCOSYSMO302019}. More broadly, the systems engineering cost estimation ecosystem includes approaches used to support planning and decision-making under uncertainty. The relevance of these foundations here is conceptual rather than one-to-one: \ac{llm} evaluation introduces different primitives (tokens, latency, and judge overhead), but the underlying logic is similar. When evidence must be collected under constraints, cost-aware trade-space reasoning is necessary to select feasible evaluation regimes and to interpret results in light of the evidence that could practically be gathered. Efforts have already begun to define cost modeling factors for \ac{ai}-augmented systems engineering and acquisition contexts \cite{madachy_SystemsAcquisitionCost_2024, Madachy_CostModelingAI_2024, madachy_GenerativeAIdrivenSystems_2025}.


\subsection{Evaluation Cost Decomposition and Design Trade-Spaces}
\label{subsec:ch2_cost_decomposition}

A useful way to reason about feasibility is to decompose evaluation cost into three multiplicative components: (i) token usage per call (input context plus output), (ii) repetition required for robustness (e.g., multiple samples, seeds, or prompt variants), and (iii) scoring overhead (human grading time or judge-model inference). Each component compounds across the experiment grid (items $\times$ conditions $\times$ replications), so evaluation design decisions that appear minor at the per-item level can become dominant constraints at scale.

These constraints create asymmetry between modalities. MCQ evidence is typically inexpensive to score and supports broad coverage and replication, whereas \ac{osq} evidence can be richer but is frequently gated by scoring overhead. In judge-based scoring, the evaluation itself becomes an additional inference workload where stability improvements may require multiple judge passes to confirm inter-correlation of judgments. Consequently, choices that emphasize depth—such as longer responses and multi-turn interactions tend to raise both generation and scoring costs, whereas choices that emphasize breadth such as adding more items, covering more topics, or evaluating more models increase the total number of model calls. Budget-aware evaluation work shows that methods can appear superior primarily because they expend more compute, and conclusions can change when compute is normalized \cite{Junlin_Wang_2024_Budget_Aware_Reasoning}. In applied settings, these economics are further influenced by access mode: organizations frequently evaluate through inferencing service offerings where pricing tiers, throughput limits, and latency targets shape both marginal cost and scalability \cite{liagkou_CostPerspectiveAdoptingLarge_2024}. Surveys of resource-efficient foundation models likewise emphasize that efficiency should be measured alongside accuracy using metrics such as memory footprint, latency, throughput, energy, and cost per token \cite{xu_SurveyResourceefficientLLMMultimodal_2024, bai_EfficiencySystematicSurveyResourceEfficient_2024}. Benchmarking work reinforces that throughput and efficiency characteristics can be first-order constraints on feasible evaluation scale \cite{Siddharth_Samsi_2023}.


\subsection{Training vs Inference Economics}
\label{subsec:ch2_train_vs_infer_cost}

The cost landscape for \acp{llm} is often divided into training and inference costs. Training costs are largely incurred by model developers and are driven by the scale of compute required to optimize model parameters over large datasets and the infrastructure required for distributed training. These costs include \ac{gpu} hours, storage and I/O, network and orchestration overhead, repeated training runs for experimentation, and the engineering effort required to operate at scale. Recent reports and analyses document the rapid growth of frontier training budgets, underscoring why training efficiency has become a growing research and engineering concern \cite{cottier_RisingCostsTraining_2024, stanforduniversity_ArtificialIntelligenceIndex_2025}. The AI Index summarizes how the training compute required by notable models has grown rapidly over time, with estimates that frontier training compute has doubled on the order of months rather than years \cite{stanforduniversity_ArtificialIntelligenceIndex_2025}. At the same time, training datasets have scaled by orders of magnitude, and training runs now routinely span many weeks to months, which tightens dependence on large-scale compute infrastructure \cite{stanforduniversity_ArtificialIntelligenceIndex_2025}.

These scaling trends increasingly require industrial-scale training environments, including large GPU clusters and data center capacity that can sustain prolonged high-power training jobs. Public disclosures around frontier deployments illustrate the resulting infrastructure scale and systems engineering complexity: xAI reported operating its Colossus cluster in Memphis at a scale of 100{,}000 NVIDIA Hopper GPUs, enabled by specialized high-throughput networking designed for hyperscale AI factories \cite{alexshapiro_NVIDIAEthernetNetworkingAccelerates_2024}. The AI Index reports sharp growth in estimated training power draw and associated emissions for frontier systems, reinforcing that training feasibility is constrained not only by dollar cost but also by access to power, cooling, and physical compute infrastructure \cite{stanforduniversity_ArtificialIntelligenceIndex_2025}. Consistent with this infrastructure push, hyperscalers continue to expand data center footprints specifically to increase AI capacity, for example Google's announced plan to invest \$40 billion in new Texas data centers through 2027 as part of its AI-driven expansion \cite{reuters_GooglePlans40Billion_2025}. These trends are reflected in the training cost trajectories shown in Figures~\ref{fig:ch2_training_cost_by_date} and \ref{fig:ch2_training_cost_by_compute}, which summarize estimated training compute costs over time and as a function of total training compute for selected systems \cite{stanforduniversity_ArtificialIntelligenceIndex_2025}. While training cost is not the dominant expense for most evaluation studies, it shapes which foundation models and variants exist, how frequently models can be updated, and the extent to which specialized domain models are economically viable.

In contrast to training, inference costs scale with usage and therefore often dominate total cost of ownership for deployed systems. For evaluation programs, inference cost is typically the most pertinent feasibility constraint because it scales directly with the number of queries, the number of models, and the number of repeated runs used for robustness. Inference cost is determined by the number of tokens processed in the input context, the number of tokens generated in the output, and serving characteristics such as batching efficiency, memory footprint, and cache behavior. The AI Index highlights that inference pricing has shifted rapidly in recent years, with large reductions in cost per token for representative capability levels \cite{stanforduniversity_ArtificialIntelligenceIndex_2025}. Figure~\ref{fig:ch2_inference_cost_by_date} summarizes these inference-cost trends over time, motivating why inference economics often determine the practical scale of evaluation grids \cite{stanforduniversity_ArtificialIntelligenceIndex_2025}. Tokenomics metrics such as throughput, end-to-end latency, time to first token, and time per output token provide an operational vocabulary for these serving constraints and for reporting evaluation feasibility \cite{dongLargeLanguageModels2024}.

Because inference dominates evaluation budgets, there is increasing interest in smaller and more resource-efficient model families and in techniques that compact or distill larger capabilities into more economical deployments. Surveys of small and efficient models emphasize that practical capability is best understood as a multi-objective trade-space over accuracy, latency, memory footprint, energy, and cost per token, rather than accuracy alone \cite{wangComprehensiveSurveySmall2024}. Complementary work on compacting and search over model design spaces highlights additional pathways to efficiency by reducing parameter count and or inference-time compute while preserving task performance \cite{jovanovic_CompactingAISearch_2024}.

Accordingly, this dissertation reports cost-relevant evidence descriptors alongside performance outcomes, enabling feasibility to be interpreted as an evaluation design dimension rather than an implicit or unreported limitation, supporting Research Question \ref{RQ3}.


\section{Synthesis of Literature Findings}
\label{sec:ch2_synthesis}

This chapter situated \acp{llm} evaluation within contemporary systems engineering practice. Across the literature, \ac{se} work products form a coupled, versioned artifact network that must remain traceable, auditable, and internally consistent across lifecycle phases. As organizations adopt digital-thread and hybrid \ac{mbse} workflows, model qualification and governance will be key.

\acp{llm} will function as actors on the digital thread, including drafting, extracting, transforming, and reconciling lifecycle artifacts. Literature shows that these benefits are paired with distinct risks. \acp{llm} can generate plausible-but-wrong content, omit constraints, and introduce subtle inconsistencies that appear coherent in isolation yet degrade the integrity of the artifact network and propagate downstream. Consequently, the adequacy of evaluation evidence depends on intended use, impact, and autonomy rather than on generic accuracy alone.

The literature also shows that evaluation pipelines have nontrivial measurement error. Results can be distorted by dataset lifecycle hazards such as contamination and saturation, as well as by protocol sensitivity including prompting, normalization choices, and versioning. For open-ended tasks, additional uncertainty arises from scoring subjectivity and judge bias (e.g., verbosity or rubric sensitivity). Benchmark scores therefore support qualification only when the evaluation instrument is explicitly characterized, controlled, and reported.

These findings motivate the methods in Chapter~\ref{ch:materials_methods}. First, because \acp{mcq} enable scalable measurement yet are sensitive to option structure and distractor design, the dissertation tests \ac{mcq} robustness under controlled structural perturbations as a measurement property under Research Question~\ref{RQ1}. Second, because \ac{mcq} and \acp{osq} provide different evidence signals, the dissertation evaluates cross-modality agreement and evidence fusion for task-aligned, risk-aware qualification under Research Question~\ref{RQ2}. Third, because qualification must be feasible under computational and judging constraints, the dissertation quantifies the cost and token-efficiency trade-space induced by modality choice, response length, and scoring design under Research Question~\ref{RQ3}.

Together, this synthesis motivates a hybrid, multi-modality evaluation approach for systems engineering, consistent with emerging practice in other domains that combine complementary evidence signals rather than relying on a single benchmark format. Chapter~\ref{ch:materials_methods} formalizes this framing through executable task formulations, controlled variants, scoring procedures, and cost accounting.
