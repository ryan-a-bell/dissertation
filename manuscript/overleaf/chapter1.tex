\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction}

\acp{llm} are increasingly used as tools for engineering work: drafting and refining technical text, summarizing references, supporting trade studies, and accelerating documentation across the digital thread \cite{deputyassistantsecretaryofdefense_DepartmentDefenseDigitalStrategy_2018, dod_digital_modernization_strategy_2019}. \ac{se} is a particularly attractive target for these capabilities because it is integrative across disciplines, heavily text-mediated, and sensitive to early work products that shape downstream commitments. However, adoption is advancing faster than the availability of defensible evidence for when an LLM should be trusted within \ac{se} workflows.

The focus of this research is to investigate how \acp{llm} should be evaluated and qualified for use in \ac{se} tasks and contexts where errors can carry significant technical, operational, or safety consequences to complex systems. Rather than treating benchmarking as an end in itself, this work treats evaluation as a qualification problem: it examines how evaluation modality, robustness properties of measurement instruments, and cost constraints contribute to defensible model qualification decisions.

In this dissertation, \emph{qualification} denotes an evidence-based decision about whether a specific \ac{llm} is acceptable for a specified \ac{se} use context, under explicit constraints and risk tolerance. A qualification claim is not a general statement that a model is ``good''; it is a bounded statement of \emph{intended use} (task class and artifact role), \emph{permitted operating conditions} (inputs, prompt scaffolds, and workflow placement), and \emph{required controls} (evaluation modality, robustness checks, and human oversight). Accordingly, evaluation results are treated as \emph{evidence} that supports or fails to support a qualification claim, rather than as an end in themselves.

\section{Motivation}\label{sec:motivation}

\subsection{Systems Engineering as a High Consequence Decision Environment}\label{subsec:motivation_se_high_consequence}

Systems engineering is a multidisciplinary approach concerned with the design, realization, and operation of complex systems over their life cycles \cite{incose_GuideSystemsEngineering_2025, Blanchard_Fabrycky_Systems_Engineering_and_Analysis_2011}. Its integrative role requires synthesizing across engineering domains and stakeholder objectives while balancing trade-offs among performance, cost, schedule, safety, and operational constraints \cite{Blanchard_Fabrycky_Systems_Engineering_and_Analysis_2011,boardman_SystemsThinkingCoping_2008}. In such settings, small misunderstandings or inconsistencies can ripple through interconnected subsystems and stakeholder interfaces, affecting performance, cost, schedule, and safety \cite{AveKlutse_Kodzo_Paaku_Kludze_2004, Samuel_K_Son_2012}.


The discipline is high-impact: empirical research suggests that \ac{se} effort is positively associated with program outcomes, with reported correlation factors as high as 80\% \cite{walden_systems_engineering_handbook_2015, elm_BusinessCaseSystemsEngineering_2012, honour_value_systems_engineering_2012}. A major reason is that defects and requirement misunderstandings are cheapest to correct early, but become substantially more expensive as they propagate into integration, test, and operations. Cost growth factors on the order of 10 to 100 times are commonly reported as issues escape early lifecycle phases \cite{walden_systems_engineering_handbook_2015, nasa_NASASystemsEngineering_2007, Tan2017EarlyLateDesign, kobren_dau_readiness_through_early_lcc_2019}. Figure~\ref{fig:lifecycle_cost_impacts} provides a representative illustration of this lifecycle cost amplification \cite{nasa_SEH25Cost_2019}. Defense acquisition guidance similarly emphasizes front-loading systems engineering and early life-cycle cost thinking to reduce downstream rework and program risk \cite{kobren_dau_readiness_through_early_lcc_2019, dau_ModelBasedSystemsEngineering_2019}.

\ac{mbse} has emerged to help manage this complexity by increasing structure, traceability, and consistency of system representations \cite{dau_ModelBasedSystemsEngineering_2019, ramos_ModelBasedSystemsEngineering_2012, borky_EffectiveModelBasedSystems_2018, vaneman_mbse_demystified_2025, delligatti_SysMLDistilledBrief_2013}. However,  adoption introduces its own challenges, including tooling and standardization friction, transitions from document-centric to model-centric practices, and the coordination overhead of maintaining models across teams and suppliers. As the community moves toward more machine-readable and text-friendly modeling ecosystems (e.g., emerging SysML v2 efforts), the opportunity grows to integrate language models with the digital thread in ways that can reduce routine burdens while preserving engineering rigor \cite{friedenthal_MovingForwardSysMLV2_2024}.

As programs expand in scope (e.g., distributed architectures, autonomous platforms, and digital twins), the cognitive load on systems engineers grows nonlinearly. Approaches such as \ac{mbse} can raise the ceiling of what is manageable by increasing structure, traceability, and consistency of system representations \cite{dau_ModelBasedSystemsEngineering_2019, ramos_ModelBasedSystemsEngineering_2012, borky_EffectiveModelBasedSystems_2018, vaneman_mbse_demystified_2025, delligatti_SysMLDistilledBrief_2013}. Even with \ac{mbse}, the cognitive limits of humans and the explosion of coordination paths in large, multi‑stakeholder system‑of‑systems programs often called "mega projects" remain bottlenecks \cite{McDermott_Generative_AI_Visualization_Managing_Megaprojects_2025}. This reality motivates the development of tools and organizational practices that offload routine burdens, preserve engineering rigor, and help teams detect unknown‑knowns despite the inherent complexity.

Because \ac{se} decisions shape downstream cost, schedule, and technical risk, even modest productivity improvements can have outsized program impact. This creates strong motivation to explore methods and tools that can reduce coordination overhead and documentation burden without degrading decision quality.

\subsection{Why LLMs are Compelling for Systems Engineering Workflows}\label{subsec:motivation_llms_compelling}

\acp{llm} are advanced AI systems trained on large text corpora to model and generate natural language \cite{commoncrawl_CommonCrawlOpen_2024}. Modern \acp{llm} typically use transformer architectures \cite{vaswani_AttentionAllYou_2017}, and have demonstrated strong performance across a wide range of knowledge and reasoning-style benchmarks \cite{lab42_ARC_2024, TidorVlad_Pricope_2025_HardML_Benchmark, Y_Thomas_Hou_2024_CompoundQA}. For \ac{se}, these capabilities map naturally to text-intensive activities such as summarization of technical references, drafting and refinement of requirements and interface descriptions, synthesis of trade-space considerations, and assistance with communication across stakeholder groups.

The appeal of LLM-enabled assistance is amplified by the ongoing shift toward digital engineering practices and the need to manage increasing volumes of engineering information across the lifecycle \cite{deputyassistantsecretaryofdefense_DepartmentDefenseDigitalStrategy_2018, dod_digital_modernization_strategy_2019, simms_adoption_of_de_within_dod_2022}. At the same time, well-documented limitations of \acp{llm} including hallucination, susceptibility to prompt injection, training data contamination, and non-transparent reasoning behaviors create a gap between apparent usefulness and defensible trust  \cite{Sanu_LLM_Limitations_2024, Matarazzo2025_Survey_LLM_Capabilities_Limits, Johnson2025_Primer_LLM_Limitations}. This gap motivates evaluation approaches that are aligned to \ac{se} task demands and that produce evidence suitable for model qualification decisions.

From a digital engineering perspective, the goal is not merely faster writing, but improved continuity and traceability across the digital thread: connecting requirements, architectures, interfaces, verification artifacts, and program decisions across the lifecycle into a digital thread \cite{Feng_Guiding_LLMs_Engineering_Design_Process_Domain_Specific_Knowledge_2025, Jaskie_Agentic_AI_Lifecycle_Traceability_Digital_Thread_2025}. This intensifies the shift from document-centric artifacts toward model-centric representations (e.g., \ac{mbse}) and structured technical knowledge that can be queried, transformed, and audited \cite{estefanSurveyModelBasedSystems2008, harvey_document_model_dont_model_document_2012, jones_digital_systems_engineering_transformation_strategy_2020}. Furthermore, \ac{se} decisions often rely on structured expert judgment and consensus-building to reconcile trade-offs, suggesting a role for LLMs in organizing deliberation and rationale under human governance \cite{bell_AutomatingAIExpert_2025}. 

\subsection{The Qualification Evidence Gap for LLM Adoption in Systems Engineering}\label{subsec:motivation_qualification_gap}

Responsible adoption of \acp{llm} in systems engineering requires evidence that model outputs are reliable under the response constraints and failure modes that matter for \ac{se} workflows. In practice, the evaluation evidence typically available to practitioners is often misaligned with domain-specific \ac{se} work products. This misalignment is most visible in the widespread reliance on \ac{mcq} benchmarking as a primary indicator of readiness, despite the fact that many \ac{se} tasks require constructive, free-form responses: explanations, justifications, trade-space arguments, and traceable reasoning artifacts. 

Recent work argues that traditional systems engineering \ac{t&e} practices, while necessary, become insufficient when engineered systems incorporate \ac{ai} components, motivating complementary approaches such as explainable AI (xAI) and counterfactual testing and evaluation \cite{raz_ExplainableAICounterfactuals_2023}. From a qualification standpoint, the objective is to produce auditable evidence that aligns to \ac{se} work products while tracking anticipated failure modes and consequence severity. This dissertation investigates this xAI gap within \ac{se} by (i) contrasting recognition-style evidence (MCQ) with constructive-style evidence (OSQ), (ii) measuring the robustness of MCQ-derived signals to semantics-preserving instrument variations like \acp{osq}, and (iii) incorporating cost and deployability constraints so that qualification protocols remain feasible.


\subsubsection{Failure modes and consequence-aware evaluation}\label{subsubsec:motivation_failure_modes}

Known \ac{llm} failure modes including hallucinations, prompt-injection vulnerabilities, contamination, and limited transparency become especially problematic when model outputs inform architectural decisions, requirements allocation, or risk assessments \cite{Sanu_LLM_Limitations_2024, Matarazzo2025_Survey_LLM_Capabilities_Limits, Johnson2025_Primer_LLM_Limitations}. Although newer reasoning-oriented models may improve the structure and traceability of generated explanations, systems engineering still requires evaluation to function as evidence for a decision: the appropriate form and strength of evidence must be selected based on task criticality, anticipated failure modes, and the degree of human oversight expected in use \cite{IntroducingOpenAIO1_2024, OpenAIO3mini_2025, deepseekai_deepseekR1-2025}. Evidence can also shift non-monotonically when models are adapted or transformed (e.g., fine-tuning improving some behaviors while degrading others), reinforcing that model variants should be treated as distinct artifacts that require task-aligned qualification rather than assumed transferability \cite{Wach_LLMTradeOffs_2025}.

\subsubsection{Evaluation modality as a first-order design choice}\label{subsubsec:motivation_modality_first_order}

Evaluation modality is not a neutral presentation detail: different formats can probe different behaviors and produce different forms of evidence. MCQ evaluation is convenient and scalable, but it primarily measures recognition and selection among provided options. \acp{osq} instead require a model to construct an answer and explanation in free text, which more directly resembles many \ac{se} work products. Because most domain benchmarks and scoreboards are dominated by MCQ-style evaluation, modality choice can materially affect what is being measured and what kinds of errors are exposed \cite{Tianle_Li_2024_BenchBuilder_ArenaHard_LLM_Benchmarking, Jin_Li_2024_Benchmarking_LLMs_EBM, Muharrem_Unal_2023_HELM_Holistic_Eval_LLMs}.

\subsubsection{Robustness limits of MCQ-only evidence}\label{subsubsec:motivation_mcq_robustness}

Even when MCQs are useful as screening instruments, MCQ-based evidence can be sensitive to question structure, option framing, and other evaluation artifacts \cite{fu_mcq_reasoning_more_confident_when_wrong_2025, zhangMitigatingEasyOption2025}. Such sensitivity creates a model qualification risk: a model may appear to pass or fail depending on design choices that are not tied to underlying \ac{se} knowledge. For defensible qualification, robustness should be treated as a property of the measurement instrument, motivating controlled MCQ variants and sensitivity analyses rather than relying on a single observed accuracy number \cite{Yue_Liu_2023_Reliability_LLMs_Program_Generation}. In particular, controlled option-rotation variants provide a semantics-preserving diagnostic that isolates artifact sensitivity from underlying domain knowledge, making robustness quantifiable and auditable within a qualification workflow.

\subsubsection{Domain-specific benchmarks as qualification infrastructure}\label{subsubsec:motivation_domain_benchmarks}

Constructing domain-specific benchmarks remains challenging because it requires representative datasets, carefully crafted tasks, and contextually relevant metrics. Other fields have already created domain-specific benchmarks, such as biomedical \cite{jin_PubMedQADatasetBiomedicalResearch_2019}, medical and clinical \cite{pal_MedMCQALargescaleMultiSubjectMultiChoice_2022}, legal \cite{guha_LegalBenchCollaborativelyBuilt_2023, chalkidis_LexGLUEBenchmarkDataset_2022}, and financial \cite{xue_FAMMABenchmarkFinancialDomain_2024, islam_FinanceBenchNewBenchmarkFinancial_2023}. Until recently, no equivalent benchmark existed for systems engineering, and generic language benchmarks do not evaluate \ac{se} concepts, \ac{se} vocabulary, \ac{mbse} syntax, lifecycle analysis, or trade-space thinking.

SysEngBench represents a benchmark tailored for evaluating \acp{llm} in systems engineering contexts \cite{bell_IntroducingSysEngBenchNovel_2024,bell_RabellSysEngBenchDatasets_2024}. The initial SysEngBench release uses the \ac{mcq} modality \cite{Bell2025SysEngBench} and subsequent analysis of varying quantization levels \cite{bell_BalancingAccuracyEfficiencyQuantization_2025}, motivating extensions that support paired OSQ items to provide complementary evidence of constructive capability when models must generate explanations and engineering rationale in free text.


\subsubsection{Cost-aware qualification and deployability constraints}\label{subsubsec:motivation_cost_aware}

Qualification decisions must also account for practical deployment constraints. Modern adaptation methods such as fine-tuning, \ac{rag}, quantization, and prompting strategies (e.g., zero-shot and few-shot) provide pathways to tailor \acp{llm} to specialized domains with different accuracy--cost trade-offs, reinforcing the need to treat efficiency as part of qualification evidence \cite{bell_BalancingAccuracyEfficiencyQuantization_2025, Wach_LLMTradeOffs_2025}. Evaluation modality and response length affect token usage, computation, and human review burden, shaping what forms of qualification evidence are feasible at scale. This dissertation therefore investigates cost differences between evaluation modalities and extends prior cost modeling work \cite{bell_BalancingAccuracyEfficiencyQuantization_2025}. These measurements also inform broader AI cost-modeling efforts and maturity frameworks for adoption in systems engineering settings 
\cite{madachy_FactoringAIAssistance_2023, madachy_SystemsAcquisitionCost_2024, Madachy_CostModelingAI_2024, madachy_GenerativeAIdrivenSystems_2025,Madachy_Bell_Longshore_2025_Generative_AI_SE_Maturity_Cost_Framework}.


\subsubsection{Guiding premises for this dissertation}\label{subsubsec:motivation_guiding_premises}

This dissertation proceeds from the following premises that organize the problem and motivate the research design:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Evaluation modality is a first-order design choice; response format changes what is being measured and what kinds of errors are exposed.
    \item MCQ-only evaluation is insufficient as qualification evidence for systems engineering work products, although it may be useful as a screening signal.
    \item Domain specificity matters for interpreting model capability in systems engineering contexts; aggregate benchmark scores may not reliably translate to \ac{se} task performance.
    \item Domain specificity is essential for interpreting model capability in systems engineering; general benchmark scores may not provide transferable qualification evidence for \ac{se} work products
    \item Qualification is contextual, not universal: different \ac{se} task classes tolerate different failure modes and require different strength of evidence.
    \item Cost and deployability constraints including response length, token usage, and efficiency under resource limits must be considered alongside measured quality.
\end{itemize}

These premises motivate a modality-aware, robustness-sensitive, and cost-aware approach to evaluation as a prerequisite for defensible model qualification in systems engineering workflows.


\section{Statement of the Problem}
The dissertation problem statement is as follows:

\emph{Current LLM evaluation practice over-relies on MCQ benchmarking, which can overstate readiness for systems engineering tasks that require models to construct answers and explanations in free text, leaving practitioners without a robust, cost-aware basis for model qualification.}

Language models should be qualified for \emph{systems engineering} based on evidence that they can reliably produce free form SE work products. The \ac{se} community stands to benefit from AI-enabled assistance, but responsible adoption depends on \ac{vandv} evidence that models perform reliably under the response constraints and failure modes that matter in SE workflows. Most benchmarking practice emphasizes recognition-style performance (e.g., MCQs), which is easy to score and scales well, yet may not reflect how a model behaves when asked to produce short engineering explanations, justify a choice, or articulate a trade-space argument in free text. \acp{osq} probe this constructive capability more directly, but they introduce practical challenges in dataset construction, scoring protocols like LLM-as-a-judge approaches, judge reliability, and computational cost.

As a result, practitioners lack a defensible and traceable basis for determining when MCQ evidence is adequate as a screening signal, when OSQ evidence is necessary to test explanatory quality under free-text constraints, and how to combine these evidence sources into qualification decisions that reflect task criticality, response requirements, and acceptable failure modes. Without a modality-aware and cost-aware qualification approach, organizations risk both over-deploying models based on optimistic proxy measures and under-utilizing efficient screening signals.

Potential benefits of addressing this problem include the following:
\begin{itemize} 
    \item A benchmark structure that supports controlled comparison across evaluation modalities for the same underlying systems engineering knowledge targets,
    \item More reliable interpretation of MCQ evidence by accounting for robustness and sensitivity to question structure, 
    \item A principled basis for combining multiple modalities as complementary qualification evidence rather than treating any single modality as sufficient, 
    \item Cost-aware qualification guidance that supports deployment under practical constraints (token usage and response length), 
    \item Improved decision support for practitioners seeking trustworthy and efficient adoption of LLMs within systems engineering workflows. 
\end{itemize}

 
\section{Research Questions}\label{sec:research_questions}

This section focuses on the \emph{unknowns} that must be resolved to treat benchmarking as a qualification problem for systems engineering.  The research questions identify what is not yet established, justify why the study is needed, and define the evidence the dissertation must produce to close the qualification gap. In this dissertation, the research questions are: 

\begin{enumerate}
    \item How robust is MCQ-based evaluation to variations in distractor/option structure in the domain of systems engineering, and what risks does this pose when MCQ accuracy is used as primary qualification evidence? \label{RQ1}
    \item How do MCQ and OSQ evaluation modalities differ in the type and strength of evidence they provide for qualifying LLMs in systems engineering, and how can evidence from multiple modalities be combined to support task-aligned, risk-aware qualification decisions for LLM use in systems engineering? \label{RQ2}
    \item How do evaluation modality and response length influence the computational cost of qualification, and what trade-offs arise between judged quality and token efficiency? \label{RQ3}
\end{enumerate}



\section{Research Objectives}\label{sec:research_objectives}

This section defines the \emph{work performed} to answer the research questions. Objectives are intentional, completable actions and provide an auditable plan of execution that links methods to results. 

\emph{The overarching objective of this research is to develop and empirically validate a modality-aware, robustness-aware, and cost-aware methodology for evaluating and qualifying LLMs for systems engineering tasks.}

Collectively, the objectives provide an auditable plan to produces and analyze evaluation evidence across modalities, tests the robustness of MCQ-derived signals, and incorporates practical cost constraints into qualification reasoning.

These research questions are investigated through the following objectives, executed via the six-phase empirical pipeline described in Chapter~\ref{ch:materials_methods}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Curate and version benchmark artifacts by acquiring SysEngBench and producing benchmark derivatives that support robustness analysis and cross-modality comparison.
    \item Generate OSQ grading scaffolds by producing canonical expected answers and structured rubrics (e.g., full/partial/none) and annotating items with taxonomy labels where applicable to support repeatable OSQ evaluation.
    \item Execute standardized and reproducible inference by evaluating a diverse set of models across all MCQ variants and OSQs using consistent task configurations, deterministic settings, and comprehensive logging of prompts and outputs.
    \item Score OSQ responses using rubric-based judging by applying an LLM-as-a-judge protocol to assign rubric scores and retaining structured scoring artifacts for analysis, including judge stability checks.
    \item Quantify robustness, cross-modality evidence patterns, and their uncertainty by using statistical tests and effect sizes to characterize MCQ sensitivity and compare MCQ versus OSQ outcomes.
    \item Evaluate token-efficiency and cost trade-offs by tracking token usage and response length, extending cost modeling to OSQ evaluation, and analyzing the quality–token trade space.
    \item Synthesize qualification guidance by interpreting results as evidence and deriving modality-aware, task-aligned recommendations for practitioners. 
\end{enumerate}


\section{Research Contributions to the Body of Knowledge}\label{sec:research_contributions}

The primary contribution of this dissertation is a decision framework for qualifying \acp{llm} for use in \ac{se} tasks. The framework treats qualification as a structured decision supported by multiple forms of evidence: (i) \emph{measurement defensibility} for whether the evaluation protocol is valid and robust enough to support decisions, (ii) \emph{modality suitability} for whether selected-response and constructed-response evaluations provide convergent evidence for the task class, and (iii) \emph{resource feasibility} for whether quality and acceptable performance is achievable within practical cost constraints.

SysEngBench functions as the instantiation used to demonstrate this qualification framework. The four contributions below should therefore be read as framework components: they define the measurement instruments, stress-test robustness, characterize modality divergence, and map performance to cost to enable efficient task-modality guidance.

\subsection{Contribution 1: Extension of SysEngBench with Multi-Format Evaluation Questions}\label{subsec:Contribution_one}

The first contribution is an extension of the SysEngBench benchmark to support paired multiple-choice and open-style question formats. By structuring evaluation items to elicit both recognition-based (MCQ) and generative responses (OSQ) to the same underlying technical concepts, this contribution establishes the foundation for systematically studying how evaluation format influences measured model capability. The resulting benchmark design enables controlled, within-task comparisons across modalities while preserving domain fidelity. 


\subsection{Contribution 2: Robustness Analysis of MCQ Evaluation Using Distractor Variation for Systems Engineering}\label{subsec:Contribution_two}

The second contribution is the investigation of the robustness of multiple-choice question (MCQ) evaluation as a source of evidence for qualifying LLMs in systems engineering contexts. It seeks to motivate the need to examine how sensitive MCQ performance is to distractor design choices and whether observed accuracy reflects stable understanding. By framing robustness as a measurement property rather than an assumed characteristic of MCQ evaluation, this contribution highlights the potential risks of overconfidence when MCQ results are interpreted without considering their structural dependencies. 


\subsection{Contribution 3: Evaluation Modality Fusion as a Basis for Model Qualification and Trustworthy Use in Systems Engineering}\label{subsec:Contribution_three}

The third contribution develops a modality-aware basis for model qualification that aligns evaluation evidence with task characteristics and risk considerations in systems engineering practice. Rather than treating different evaluation formats as interchangeable, it motivates the use of multiple modalities—interpreted jointly—as complementary sources of evidence for assessing model capability.  This contribution frames model qualification as a systems engineering decision problem, in which task criticality, free-text response requirements, and acceptable failure modes inform how evaluation results should be combined to support trustworthy deployment within SE workflows. 


\subsection{Contribution 4: Token-Efficient Qualification: Cost-Aware Response Length Trade-offs in High-Performing Models}\label{subsec:Contribution_four}

The fourth contribution is the framing of model evaluation and qualification as a trade-space that extends beyond accuracy to include computational cost and response length. It motivates the need to distinguish between models that perform well in principle and those that do so more efficiently, with equivalent accuracy for less tokens. By introducing token efficiency as a qualification consideration, this contribution establishes cost-aware evaluation as a component of LLM adoption in systems engineering environments, particularly where resource constraints, cost, or scaling considerations are present.

\section{Structure of the Dissertation}\label{sec:structure_dissertation}
The outline of this dissertation manuscript serves as a road-map of work to be shared. The dissertation began with the Front Matter, which presents the abstract along with tables and figure lists.

Chapter 1 establishes the motivation for the research, defines the problem, articulates the guiding research questions and objectives, outlines the contributions to the \ac{se} body of knowledge, and concludes with how the dissertation is organized.

Chapter 2 reviews the literature at the intersection of systems engineering, \ac{mbse}, and \ac{llm} evaluation, reframing benchmarking as a qualification problem. It establishes evaluation modality, robustness, and cost as first-order considerations when LLM outputs inform systems engineering decisions, and identifies the absence of a multi-modality \ac{se} benchmark supporting free-text responses such as \acp{osq}. The chapter derives the conceptual requirements that motivate the empirical meta-evaluation methodology developed in subsequent chapters.

Chapter 3 details the overall research framework, from dataset construction to evaluation procedures and computational methods. It specifies the analytical metrics and methods used to ensure rigorous and reproducible results.

Chapter 4 reports the experimental findings, presenting model performance on both \acp{mcq} and \acp{osq} while comparing outcomes across modalities. Statistical analysis and a tokenomics analysis of the results is also shared.

Chapter 5 interprets the results and discusses implications for SE benchmarking methodology and model qualification. It addresses the research limitations and outlines opportunities for future research.

Chapter 6 provides practical applications of the research, summarizes key contributions, and reflects on the broader impact to the Systems Engineering community with final thoughts.
