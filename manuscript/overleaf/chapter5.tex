\chapter{Discussion}\label{ch:discussion}

This chapter interprets the results presented in Chapter \ref{ch:results} through the lens established in Chapter \ref{ch:introduction}: the need for trustworthy and cost-aware use of \acp{llm} in \ac{se}, and the role of benchmarking as a prerequisite for reliable adoption. The discussion is organized into three parts. 

First, it interprets the empirical findings across evaluation modalities and token usage behavior. Second, it translates those findings into implications for benchmarking methodology, including task-modality alignment and evaluation robustness. Finally, it identifies key limitations and outlines future directions for extending this work.


\section{Interpretation of Findings}\label{sec:interpretation_findings}
\subsection{Interpreting MCQ to OSQ Conversion Suitability and Filtering Outcomes}

A central enabling step of this research is the transformation of \acp{mcq} into \acp{osq} while preserving semantic intent and domain relevance. The suitability score distribution reported in Chapter \ref{ch:results} indicates that conversion feasibility is not uniform across the source dataset. The observed split has a substantial majority of questions meeting the inclusion threshold for OSQ conversion (845 questions, 73.9\%) and a non-trivial minority rejected (299 questions, 26.1\%), shown in Figure \ref{fig:distribution-of-suitability}.

The distribution is concentrated at the high end. The accepted region (scores $\geq 7$) contains roughly three-quarters of the questions, and the mode occurs at score 9, which accounts for a large fraction of the dataset. By contrast, rejected items are distributed across scores 1--6 with comparatively smaller bins, indicating that low scores occur less frequently than high scores.

Conversion outcomes also vary by topic area. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:osq-conversion-category-distribution}, a small number of categories, most notably Design Standards, DoDAF, Requirements, and Project Management, contribute the largest volumes, while many other categories appear only a few times. This long-tailed category structure means that overall acceptance and rejection counts are dominated by the most common categories, and per-category rates for less frequent topics are based on limited samples.

The overall acceptance rate indicates that many domain-specific SE MCQs can be re-expressed as textual-response items without changing the underlying knowledge or capability being assessed. This provides a practical path for expanding an existing benchmark into richer evaluation modalities without rebuilding the dataset from scratch. The rejected subset marks identifiable boundaries for conversion, including items where the multiple-choice options provide essential scaffolding (e.g., ambiguity resolved only by options) or where discriminative power depends on the distractor structure rather than the stem alone.


\subsection{Interpreting MCQ Positional Effects and Robustness Metrics}

Position effects arise from an interaction between the model and the MCQ presentation, and can therefore be interpreted as (a) a measurement artifact, (b) a behavioral bias of the model, or (c) a combination of both. These effects are revealed when accuracy is not invariant under controlled perturbations of the MCQ form (e.g., answer key position, option ordering, or distractor configuration).

Accordingly, the MCQ results provide two complementary forms of evidence:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item Outcome performance signals reflected in accuracy differences across models.
    \item Robustness signals reflected in sensitivity to controlled perturbations of the MCQ form (e.g., answer position and distractor configuration).
\end{enumerate}

The observed spread in MCQ accuracy across models (approximately 0.56 to 0.97; Figure~\ref{fig:mcq_position_heatmap}) indicates that the dataset meaningfully separates capability levels under the evaluated prompt constraints. This supports the use of MCQs as an efficient instrument for broad capability stratification and regression testing, particularly when the goal is to detect shifts in overall correctness with low inference and scoring overhead.

However, the positional analysis motivates a more careful interpretation of what MCQ accuracy measures. If performance varies systematically with answer position or option ordering, the evaluation may partially reflect sensitivity to presentation cues rather than only domain understanding. The per-position accuracy heatmap (Figure~\ref{fig:mcq_position_heatmap}) shows that position dependence is not uniform across models: some models exhibit tightly clustered accuracies across A--D, while others show pronounced per-position differences, including large drops concentrated in specific answer key positions. The statistical summaries in Table~\ref{tab:position_robustness_results} reflect the same heterogeneity by partitioning models into \emph{Moderate bias}, \emph{Mixed evidence}, and \emph{No bias} groups based on the joint behavior of hypothesis tests (chi-square and Friedman) and effect-size measures (Cram\'er's $V$ and Kendall's $W$).

From a benchmarking perspective, systematic performance differences by answer position (Figure~\ref{fig:mcq_position_heatmap}) constitute a threat to comparability unless controlled. If two models differ in their sensitivity to presentation cues, then raw MCQ accuracy can partially reflect format brittleness rather than only domain understanding. This motivates procedural controls (e.g., randomization or rotation of option positions) as well as reporting practices that expose variance across perturbations rather than using a single point estimate. The position-bias evidence summarized in Table~\ref{tab:position_robustness_results} is characterizes a measurement condition that can confound model-to-model comparisons if left unmeasured.

From a model-behavior perspective, position sensitivity provides a diagnostic of brittleness. Models that exhibit stronger position sensitivity may be more prone to superficial heuristics that can inflate confidence in settings where the answer is not anchored by deep reasoning. This concern aligns with the trustworthiness framing in Chapter \ref{ch:introduction}, where deployment risk is driven not only by average accuracy but also by failure modes and robustness under small input changes.

Finally, \acp{mcq} trade expressiveness for efficiency. \acp{mcq} can reward recognition and eliminate the need to articulate rationale, which is appropriate for certain SE knowledge checks (definitions, process relationships, categorical mappings). These robustness findings directly address \textbf{RQ1} and support \textbf{Contribution~2} (robustness analysis of MCQ evaluation using distractor variation) by demonstrating that positional sensitivity is a measurable and non-trivial property of MCQ-based benchmarks in the SE domain.

\subsection{Interpreting OSQ Performance}

The OSQ results introduce a different measurement regime. Models must generate content, and scoring reflects rubric-based quality rather than discrete option selection. This shift inherently changes what "success" represents. A correct OSQ response can encode partial correctness, incomplete reasoning, or correct conclusions with weak justification, depending on rubric design and judging fidelity.

OSQ outcomes are summarized using three complementary views: (i) per-model OSQ total-score distributions for two judge models (Figures~\ref{fig:osq_score_dist_gptoss} and~\ref{fig:osq_score_dist_gpt5mini}), (ii) a grouped stacked-bar decomposition of mean rubric totals and their five component dimensions by model and judge (Figure~\ref{fig:inter-judge-grading-histogram}), and (iii) inter-judge agreement diagnostics via a grading-difference histogram and a rank-correlation summary (Figure~\ref{fig:inter-judge-grading-histogram} and Table~\ref{tab:interjudge_corr}). In the distribution plots, OSQ total scores are shown on a common 0--100 scale with a shared passing reference at 70, enabling direct judge-to-judge comparison of distribution shape, spread, and threshold crossing behavior.

Inter-judge Spearman correlation is $\rho$ = 0.9012 (n = 16,051), which indicates strong monotonic agreement in ordering across the shared scored responses. If one judge tends to rate a model’s responses higher than another model’s responses, the other judge usually preserves that ordering. That makes the pair credible given the inter-judge rank consistency, for comparative evaluation, especially when downstream analyses depend on ranks, relative comparisons, or “top group vs bottom group” separation.  Judging failures were rare relative to evaluation volume, and the small number of parse errors is summarized separately (Table~\ref{tab:osq_judging_errors_combined}).

Taken together, these diagnostics support treating OSQ scores as a stable comparative signal under multi-judge scoring, while reserving stronger claims about absolute thresholds for judge-conditioned reporting. Accordingly, OSQ results are used primarily to establish relative model quality and to expose rubric-dimension tradeoffs that are not observable under MCQ evaluation, with the two-judge setup functioning as an explicit robustness check on the grading process.


\subsection{Interpreting the Cross-Modality Relationship Between MCQ and OSQ Evaluations }

The cross-modality analyses collectively indicate that MCQ and OSQ capture overlapping but non-equivalent evidence about model performance on the matched-question subset. At the aggregate level, the MCQ-OSQ scatter plots (Figures~\ref{fig:mcq-vs-osq-global} and~\ref{fig:mcq-vs-osq-zoomed}) establish that the modalities are positively associated while also exhibiting a persistent deviation from one-to-one agreement. The fitted regression and reported association strength (\(R^2=0.647\)) support the interpretation that models that score higher under MCQ tend to score higher under OSQ as well, implying a shared underlying capability signal across selection and generation regimes. However, the visual dominance of points below the \(y=x\) perfect-agreement line in both the global and zoomed panels indicates a systematic modality gap. For most models, OSQ correctness is lower than MCQ accuracy on the same matched items. The zoomed view reinforces that this pattern is not driven only by weak models or a wide axis range; the downward shift persists within the high-performing cluster, implying that the gap reflects a structural property of the measurement regimes rather than a corner-case artifact.

The per-model delta table (Table~\ref{tab:per_model_deltas}) sharpens this conclusion by showing that the modality gap is not a constant offset but varies substantially by model, with \(\Delta_i\) spanning from small differences to large separations. This variability implies that a single calibration factor cannot reconcile the modalities and that the extent to which MCQ overstates OSQ-relevant performance is model-dependent. In qualification terms, \(\Delta_i\) functions as a modality-risk indicator: large deltas identify cases where MCQ evidence provides limited support for claims about generative quality, while smaller deltas indicate comparatively closer alignment between selection-based correctness and rubric-scored generation. Together with the scatter plots, these deltas support interpreting modality mismatch as an empirically measurable property of model behavior.

Dimension-level evidence further explains why this gap persists. The combined multi-judge rubric-dimension scatter (Figure~\ref{fig:mcq-vs-osq-dimension-multijudge}) shows that the relationship between MCQ accuracy and OSQ outcomes differs by rubric component. The strongest alignment occurs for correctness- and coverage-oriented dimensions (e.g., Technical Accuracy and Completeness), while weaker alignment appears for presentation-oriented dimensions such as Clarity \& Organization. The corresponding dimension-wise regression statistics (Table~\ref{tab:mcq_osq_rubric_corr_mj}) formalize this pattern: dimensions with higher \(R^2\) are those most directly tied to factual correctness and completeness, whereas Clarity \& Organization exhibits the weakest explanatory power. This distinction supports a substantive interpretation of what each modality measures: MCQ performance tracks knowledge recognition and distractor discrimination, which correlates strongly with correctness-like rubric components, but MCQ is structurally incapable of directly measuring aspects of generative quality such as organization, justification quality, and professional communication.

The judge correlation table (Table~\ref{tab:mcq_osq_corr}) indicates that cross-modality association is stable across the two judges, with Pearson and Spearman correlations remaining in a similar range for each judge and for their average. This supports interpreting the observed MCQ-OSQ relationship as a property of the modalities rather than an artifact of a single grader's idiosyncratic calibration. At the same time, the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank results (Table~\ref{tab:wilcoxon}) provide inferential confirmation that the modality gap is systematic. The consistently small \(p\)-values across judges, along with positive median and mean paired differences, reject the null hypothesis of no systematic separation between MCQ and OSQ on the matched subset. Importantly, differences in the estimated median and mean separation between judges imply that judge calibration can affect the \emph{magnitude} of the gap, but not its \emph{existence}; this motivates judge-conditioned reporting when absolute thresholds are used while still supporting robust conclusions about directional modality separation.

Finally, the cross-modality classification results (Table~\ref{tab:mcq_osq_results_summary}) convert these statistical and visual findings into decision-relevant categories. Models assigned to the \emph{Divergent} group exhibit a combination of low association and large paired differences, indicating that MCQ and OSQ provide materially different evidence for those cases; MCQ-only evaluation is therefore least defensible when the evaluation claim depends on generative adequacy, explanation quality, or completeness. The larger \emph{Mixed evidence} group indicates that many models exhibit moderate association paired with non-trivial differences, implying that MCQ can contain predictive signal for OSQ but does not provide interchangeable evidence. In synthesis, these artifacts support a clear qualification stance: MCQ and OSQ share a common capability signal and therefore correlate, but OSQ imposes additional demands that systematically lower measured performance relative to MCQ on matched items, with the size of the gap varying across models and rubric dimensions. Consequently, modality choice should be treated as part of the evaluation claim itself (selection accuracy versus generative quality), and dual-modality evaluation is most justified when conclusions require robustness to modality-dependent failure modes or depend on rubric dimensions that MCQ cannot directly observe. This evidence directly addresses \textbf{RQ2} and provides the empirical foundation for \textbf{Contribution~3} (evaluation modality fusion as a basis for model qualification).

\subsection{Interpreting Tokenomics and the Cost of Quality}

The tokenomics results show that inference cost is inseparable from output quality: models differ not only in how many tokens they emit, but in how reliably those tokens produce rubric-satisfying content. Interpreting token usage as a deployment resource requires looking at response length within each quality tier, how often each tier occurs, and the resulting cost–quality tradeoffs. Across Figures~\ref{fig:tokenomics_distribution}, \ref{fig:tokenomics_length_vs_quality}, and \ref{fig:pareto_quality_vs_token_usage}, the central implication is that cost is better understood as \emph{expected tokens per acceptable response}.

At the response level, Figure~\ref{fig:tokenomics_distribution} indicates that higher quality tiers often shift toward higher token counts, consistent with stronger OSQ answers requiring additional explanation, structure, and completeness. However, substantial dispersion and frequent high-token outliers across multiple tiers show that verbosity is an imperfect proxy for excellence. Long responses also appear in lower-quality bins, implying that some token expenditure reflects redundancy or drift rather than improved rubric compliance. This indicates that response length is not only about typical size; the long tail matters, as occasional very long outputs can disproportionately increase runtime and cost. 

Figure~\ref{fig:tokenomics_length_vs_quality} shifts the emphasis from how long responses are to how often they are usable. Models with large \textit{Poor} shares imply that a substantial fraction of token spend yields low-utility outputs that would require rework, retries, or human intervention, while models with large \textit{Excellent} shares convert token expenditure into deployable artifacts more reliably. Operationally, this quality-yield view is more decision-relevant than raw length because it governs the expected number of attempts needed to obtain an acceptable answer and therefore the effective cost of a successful outcome.

These relationships culminate in Figure~\ref{fig:pareto_quality_vs_token_usage}, which frames model choice as a multi-objective trade space. The Pareto frontier indicates that several models represent efficient operating points. Moving toward higher mean judge scores generally entails higher median token usage, while reducing token usage entails accepting lower quality. Dominated models are those that are worse on both axes and are difficult to justify absent external constraints (e.g., privacy, availability, or deployment requirements). Overall, the tokenomics evidence motivates reporting efficiency frontiers as part of qualification, because they make explicit how much token cost is required to achieve a given level of judged OSQ quality.


\subsection{Interpreting INCOSE Task Categories Evaluation Modalities}
\label{subsec:interpret_modality_task_mapping}

The INCOSE-category decomposition shows that modality choice is category-dependent: the same model can appear highly competent under MCQ while exhibiting materially lower OSQ performance on the matched questions. BBecause the aligned MCQ--OSQ dataset pairs the same questions across modalities, the observed gaps are attributable to modality differences rather than to variation in question composition. Collectively, Figures~\ref{fig:mcq_accuracy_by_incose_category}--\ref{fig:mcq_vs_osq_scatter_regions} and Table~\ref{tab:qual_modality_class_by_incose} indicate that MCQ and OSQ provide overlapping but non-equivalent evidence, and that the strength of that mismatch varies systematically by INCOSE category.

The MCQ and OSQ heatmaps establish how measurement resolution changes with modality. The MCQ heatmap (Figure~\ref{fig:mcq_accuracy_by_incose_category}) shows strong stratification by model and category difficulty but also clear ceiling effects in easier categories, where repeated high-90\% to 100\% cells compress differences among high-capability models and indicate local benchmark saturation (i.e., the instrument no longer differentiates performance because many models reach the scoring ceiling). 

The OSQ heatmap (Figure~\ref{fig:osq_accuracy_by_incose_category}) exhibits broader dispersion and lower minima, especially for weaker models and harder categories, suggesting that open-ended scoring exposes failure modes that MCQ can partially mask. Interpreted jointly, these figures support the claim that MCQ is most informative where categories avoid saturation, while OSQ retains discriminative power by requiring generative adequacy rather than recognition alone.

The delta analysis quantifies the category dependence of the modality gap. The category-level mean deltas (Figure~\ref{fig:mcq_osq_delta_by_incose_category}) are positive for all categories, indicating a universal MCQ advantage on matched items, but the magnitude varies widely from $+7.9\%$ (Architecture Definition Process) to $+19.6\%$ (Modeling and Simulation). The categories with the largest deltas---Modeling and Simulation, Modeling Frameworks and Methods, Decision Management and Tradespace Analysis, and System Safety Engineering---tend to involve synthesis-heavy, judgment-intensive knowledge where constructing a rationale is central to competence. In these areas, the MCQ format is least representative of the underlying task demand, and MCQ-only evaluation would provide the most misleading qualification evidence precisely where the stakes of generative competence are highest.


These results suggest the MCQ–OSQ discrepancy cannot be modeled as a constant offset; instead, it is category-dependent, with larger gaps concentrated in categories that place greater requirements on generative explanation.  The scatter with decision regions (Figure~\ref{fig:mcq_vs_osq_scatter_regions}) shows that while category-level MCQ and OSQ are strongly associated (\(R^2=0.811\)), correlation does not imply interchangeability: the conservative \emph{MCQ-sufficient} band requires both high MCQ accuracy and small cross-modality separation, while categories outside that band are those where the evaluation claim depends on modality and therefore benefits from dual-modality evidence.

Table~\ref{tab:qual_modality_class_by_incose} converts these patterns into actionable guidance. Categories labeled \emph{Dual-modality required} coincide with the largest mean deltas and substantial high-divergence rates, indicating that MCQ-only evaluation would frequently misrepresent OSQ-relevant competence in those areas. Seven categories are classified as \textit{dual-modality required} ($|\overline{\Delta}_c| \geq 0.15$): Modeling and Simulation ($+19.6\%$), Modeling Frameworks and Methods ($+18.6\%$), Decision Management, Analysis of Alternatives, and Tradespace Analysis ($+18.2\%$), System Safety Engineering ($+17.6\%$), Configuration and Information Management ($+16.5\%$), Usability and Human Systems Integration ($+15.7\%$), and Risk and Opportunity Management ($+15.4\%$). For these categories, MCQ-only evaluation carries substantial risk of overestimating model readiness for tasks that inherently require constructed justification rather than option selection. 

Categories labeled \emph{Dual-modality recommended} still exhibit positive deltas and nontrivial divergence, implying that OSQ adds assurance even where MCQ is more predictive. Notably, no category reaches the \textit{MCQ-sufficient} classification ($|\Delta| < 5\%$ with MCQ accuracy $\geq 85\%$), which means the entire SysEngBench taxonomy exhibits meaningful modality sensitivity.

This result empirically validates the Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction} premise that MCQ-only benchmarking is insufficient across the full scope of SE knowledge areas, and it provides the category-level granularity needed for task-aligned qualification decisions (\textbf{RQ2}, supporting \textbf{C1} and \textbf{C3}).


\section{Implications for SE Benchmarking Methodology and Model Qualification}\label{sec:implications_benchmarking}

Taken together, the evidence indicates that evaluation modality, robustness controls, and resource accounting are required to determine the scope and defensibility of a performance claim. In particular, the aligned MCQ--OSQ comparisons demonstrate that selection accuracy and rubric-scored generation quality provide overlapping but non-interchangeable evidence, and that the magnitude of cross-modality separation is category dependent. The tokenomics analyses further show that performance must be interpreted jointly with cost, because models occupy different quality-cost regimes and can exhibit long-tail token usage. These findings motivate a qualification-oriented benchmarking stance in which the minimum evidence required to support a claim depends on (i) the construct being claimed (recognition vs.\ generative adequacy), (ii) the task family, and (iii) the operational constraints under which the model will be used. These interpretations motivate the implications presented next, which translate the findings into recommendations for defensible benchmarking practice for systems engineers. 



% ---------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Implication 1: Modality is part of the evaluation design}
\label{subsec:ch5_imp_claim_tiers}

A central implication is that modality choice is part of the claim being made. MCQ outcomes support claims about \emph{recognition competence} under constrained response form, while OSQ outcomes support claims about \emph{generative competence} under rubric-scored explanations. Therefore, benchmarks should explicitly state which claim tier is supported by the reported evidence:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Tier 1: Recognition competence (MCQ).} Evidence supports the ability to select correct answers under fixed options and distractors.
    \item \textbf{Tier 2: Generative competence (OSQ).} Evidence supports the ability to generate rubric-satisfying responses.
    \item \textbf{Tier 3: Hybrid competence.} Evidence integrates MCQ and OSQ outcomes and includes robustness and feasibility checks (e.g., position effects, judge sensitivity, and cost budgets) to support operational constraints. 
\end{itemize}

In this framing, it is methodologically inadvisable to treat MCQ and OSQ as interchangeable measures of capability without explicitly constraining the claim to the tier supported by the evidence.

% ---------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Implication 2: Measurement instrument robustness must be recorded}
\label{subsec:ch5_imp_instrument_robustness}

MCQ results should not be treated as fully objective unless robustness to presentation artifacts is measured. Position effects and distractor configuration can induce systematic shifts in observed MCQ accuracy, creating comparability risk when different models respond differently to the same superficial perturbations. Therefore, benchmarks should include and report robustness checks as part of the default MCQ protocol (e.g., rotated variants, effect sizes, and whether positional sensitivity is present). 

Similarly, OSQ scoring should not be treated as a single "judge truth", judge sensitivity needs to be quantified. Multi-judge scoring, agreement statistics, and explicit reporting of judge-dependent differences provide a minimum reliability scaffold for generative evaluation. Robustness reporting should be treated as a qualification requirement since it bounds how much the reported metric depends on the measurement instrument rather than the model's underlying capability.

% ---------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Implication 3: Minimum evidence bundles should be task-family aware}
\label{subsec:ch5_imp_evidence_bundles}

Cross-modality separation is category-dependent, so the evidence needed to support a defensible claim must also be category-dependent. Where MCQ and OSQ diverge more strongly, MCQ-only results are more likely to overstate deployable competence and OSQ becomes necessary to validate generative adequacy. Where divergence is smaller, MCQ can serve as an efficient screening instrument, provided robustness controls are applied.

For this reason, it is recommended to report results as \emph{evaluation profiles} rather than single summary scores. Each profile defines the minimum measurements required to support a tier of claim for a task family:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Profile A (Screening):} MCQ accuracy + MCQ robustness checks (rotation/distractors) with variance reporting.
    \item \textbf{Profile B (Dual-modality qualification):} Profile A + OSQ rubric scoring + multi-judge reliability + score distributions.
    \item \textbf{Profile C (Cost-aware qualification):} Profile B + tokenomics (length distributions, quality yield, and an efficiency frontier).

\end{itemize}

These profiles should be selected based on the claim scope and the category's modality sensitivity. Profile~C is not required for every study; however, Tier~3 deployment claims are not defensible without explicit feasibility evidence, and Profile~C provides the minimum cost-aware scaffold needed to evaluate whether a quality claim remains feasible under budget and latency constraints.


% ---------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Implication 4: Reporting standards should support auditability and reuse}
\label{subsec:ch5_imp_reporting_standards}

To make benchmark results auditable and reusable, we recommend a standardized reporting package that mirrors the claim tiers and evidence bundles above. At minimum, a benchmark should report:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Modality-separated outcomes:} MCQ accuracy and OSQ score distributions, not only aggregated means.
    \item \textbf{Cross-modality evidence:} deltas and a decision mapping that indicates whether modalities are interchangeable, correlated-but-not-interchangeable, or divergent for the target task family.
    \item \textbf{Robustness controls:} MCQ artifact sensitivity (e.g., answer position) and OSQ judge sensitivity (e.g., agreement).
    \item \textbf{Tokenomics:} token usage distributions by quality tier and an efficiency frontier linking quality to cost.
    \item \textbf{Traceability artifacts:} run configuration, prompts, rubric definitions, and parsing/error rates sufficient to reproduce scoring.
\end{enumerate}

This reporting package ensures that downstream users can (i) select an evaluation modality consistent with their intended claim, (ii) compare models without conflating instrument artifacts with capability, and (iii) reason about cost--quality tradeoffs rather than treating cost as an afterthought.

% ---------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Implication 5: Benchmarks must anticipate saturation and evolve}
\label{subsec:ch5_imp_saturation}

Finally, the heatmap patterns imply that portions of the benchmark can saturate under MCQ and, in limited cases, under OSQ. Saturation reduces discriminative power and can create misleading impressions of equivalence among strong models. Therefore, benchmark maintenance should include (i) periodic difficulty rebalancing, (ii) targeted item refresh for saturated categories, and (iii) reporting of saturation indicators (e.g., fraction of 100\% cells, ceiling proximity). In a qualification framing, saturation is a governance issue because it limits the benchmark's ability to support defensible comparative claims over time.

\subsection{Summary of implications}
\label{subsec:ch5_imp_summary}

The implications above establish a qualification-oriented stance for benchmarking: modality defines the scope of the performance claim, robustness controls bound measurement sensitivity, evaluation profiles provide minimum evidence requirements conditional on SE task family, reporting standards enable auditability, and saturation motivates benchmark governance and refresh. Together, these points specify \emph{what} must be measured and reported to make defensible claims about \acp{llm} in \ac{se}, but they do not yet prescribe \emph{how} practicing systems engineers should operationalize these requirements in day-to-day workflows. Accordingly, the final chapter, Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion}, translates these methodological implications into practitioner-facing guidance, along with a summary of contributions and closing thoughts.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}\label{sec:limits_and_future_work}

This section discusses the limitations of the research and outlines future research directions. Limitations are organized along the following dimensions: scope, dataset construction, execution constraints, model behavior, and evaluation reliability. Future work synthesizes research findings with limitations into a forward-looking, benchmark-informed systems engineering workflow.

\subsection{Scope and Temporal Constraints}

This dissertation is scoped to evaluate \acp{llm} for systems engineering tasks through comparative \ac{mcq} and \ac{osq} benchmarking, including hybrid approaches and tokenomics analysis. The work is anchored in foundational systems engineering concepts drawn from the INCOSE Handbook and related activities such as requirements analysis, conceptual design, risk management, and \ac{mbse}. The overarching goal is to make SysEngBench a community-driven, extensible benchmarking resource for systems engineering, supplying a methodological template for both its ongoing development and for those creating private or derivative benchmarks.

The research is deliberately bounded in both concept and method. It examines model performance and evaluation modalities with benchmark modalities are confined to \ac{mcq} and \ac{osq} formats and their hybrid combinations; multi-modal inputs and agentic workflows remain outside the present scope. From a data perspective, SysEngBench relies available systems engineering sources and does not incorporate proprietary industrial or government datasets. Temporally, the evaluation reflects the 2024 to late-2025 \ac{llm} landscape, recognizing that future models may shift absolute performance while leaving the analytical framework relevant.

\subsection{Dataset and Taxonomy Limitations}

Several dataset and taxonomy limitations motivate future extensions to SysEngBench and the evaluation of \ac{ai} models for Systems Engineers.

\subsubsection{Lack of Difficulty and Cognitive Depth Annotation}

SysEngBench lacks explicit annotations for question difficulty (e.g., easy, medium, hard) and for cognitive depth, such as Bloom's taxonomy \cite{blooms_taxonomy_1956}. This gap was partially addressed in the MCQ-to-OSQ conversion prompt (\ref{app:converter_prompt_template}) to preserve data for future work; however, this coverage applies only to converted items. MCQs that were not converted were neither labeled nor human reviewed.

As a result, the benchmark cannot report performance conditioned on difficulty strata, nor can it isolate whether observed performance differences are driven by genuine capability variation or by uneven mixtures of question difficulty across categories. Without difficulty or cognitive depth labels, SysEngBench may under-characterize where models provide meaningful systems engineering value.

Prior work has demonstrated the value of structuring evaluation benchmarks by Bloom levels for deeper model assessment \cite{Liangtai_Sun_2023_SciEval_Benchmark}. Extending SysEngBench to include Bloom-level labeling would enable more diagnostic analyses, such as distinguishing recognition-dominated MCQ performance from OSQ responses that reflect deeper reasoning and synthesis.

\subsubsection{Category Imbalance and Coverage Skew}

Category representation remains uneven across the benchmark. Some systems engineering concept categories contain substantially more items than others, which can bias aggregate conclusions toward heavily represented topics. This imbalance is particularly consequential when categories differ in inherent difficulty. Future SysEngBench releases should expand sparse categories and publish per-category performance summaries to reduce the risk of overgeneralization from aggregate results.

\subsection{Evaluation and Execution Constraints}

Several limitations arose from practical considerations during model execution rather than from the framework itself.

\subsubsection{Token Budget Constraints}

The initial evaluation configuration enforced a maximum response length of 10,000 tokens. Under this constraint, several thinking-oriented models frequently exhausted the token budget before producing a final response suitable for scoring. To distinguish truncation artifacts from genuinely non-terminating behavior, the maximum response budget was decreased to 500 tokens for a targeted subset of models exhibiting repeated truncations or incomplete outputs.

Despite this adjustment, some models continued to produce operationally unusable outputs, such as indefinite generation or prolonged internal reasoning without a terminal answer. Models that consistently provided these outputs were omitted from quantitative analyses to preserve comparability and to avoid scoring artifacts driven by incomplete responses rather than task competence.

\subsubsection{Inference Timeouts and Service Reliability}

A small number of evaluation responses within less than 4 runs experienced timeouts that were attributable to operational conditions rather than to specific model behaviors. These timeouts are attributed to high concurrency (multi-threaded execution with a large number of simultaneous requests) or due to inference-service-level effects in OpenRouter (e.g., transient throttling, queuing, or upstream routing latency) during peak load. Fewer than 27 such occurrences were observed across all models and they were clustered during high-parallelism intervals. Timeout responses were deterministically re-run with temperature set to zero and completed successfully, indicating that the failures were transient execution artifacts rather than systematic model limitations.

\subsubsection{Idempotence and Deterministic Re-Execution}

The evaluation pipeline and associated code were designed to be idempotent. Aside from transient networking failures, repeated executions under identical configurations yielded consistent results. This supports the reproducibility of reported findings and isolates observed variability to model behavior rather than to execution nondeterminism.

\subsection{Model Behavior and Reasoning Control Limitations}

\subsubsection{Thinking-Model Failure Modes}

A subset of model runs were omitted due to response-format incompatibilities or pathological generation behaviors. Although the \ac{mcq} and \ac{osq} pipelines were designed to support broad model coverage, the empirical results reflect only those models whose outputs could be parsed and scored reliably under the study conditions. This introduces a selection effect favoring models that both produce responses within expected structural bounds and remain stable under the prompting and token limits used.

For models that expose explicit reasoning traces, internal "thinking" content counted toward the response token budget. In cases where a model remained in a reasoning phase without emitting a final answer, no scorable output was produced, resulting in low or null scores. These behaviors were initially observed when running a test batch of fewer than 50 evaluation items per affected model, where reliability in producing bounded, terminal responses remained insufficient despite attempts at mitigation.

\subsubsection{Incompatibilities with Reasoning Control Flags}

Additional limitations arose from heterogeneous support for reasoning-control parameters across inference stacks, particularly when running models with Ollama. Attempts to suppress or hide reasoning traces varied across model choices, including Phi-4, including:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Standard settings:} models emitting reasoning prior to final answers broke naive MCQ parsing by introducing early option tokens during explanation.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{think=false}:} some models continued to emit reasoning traces, indicating incomplete compatibility with inference controls.
    \item \textbf{\texttt{hidethinking=true}:} suppressing reasoning traces sometimes resulted in empty-string outputs that were not scorable if response went over the token limit.
\end{itemize}


\subsubsection{Selection Effects Introduced by Model Filtering}

The decision was made to exclude open-source thinking models hosted in environments where reasoning could not be reliably controlled or parsed. The focus of this dissertation is meta-evaluation methodology for systems engineering benchmarks rather than benchmarking reasoning trace behavior itself.

Reasoning-capable proprietary API models (e.g., GPT-5) typically withhold raw chain-of-thought by default, exposing only final answers and, optionally, reasoning summaries \cite{openai_ReasoningModels_2025}. This reduces exposure to leakage-style failure modes (e.g., revealing unsafe intermediate deliberation) and encouraged treating deliberation as non-observable across evaluated systems.


\subsection{Scoring, Parsing, and Judge Reliability}

\subsubsection{MCQ Parsing Fragility}

In MCQ settings, explicit reasoning traces occasionally caused incorrect answer extraction when models enumerated options during explanation. Naive regular-expression approaches could capture an early option token rather than the final selection. Correcting this would require more robust parsing strategies (e.g., explicit final-answer anchoring) or manual intervention, both of which were deemed outside the scope of the framework used in this study.

\subsubsection{OSQ Non-Termination and Empty Outputs}

During OSQ evaluation runs, some models failed to produce judge-able responses due to indefinite generation, token exhaustion, or suppressed outputs yielding empty strings. These behaviors prevented reliable scoring and motivated model exclusion of affected outputs from aggregate analyses.

\subsubsection{Implications for Automated Benchmarking Pipelines}

These parsing and termination issues highlight limitations in current automated benchmarking pipelines when applied to heterogeneous model behaviors. They reinforce the importance of explicit output contracts (e.g. JSON), termination guarantees, and parsing robustness in future benchmark designs.


\subsection{Future Directions}

This dissertation establishes a foundation for qualification-oriented evaluation of large language models in systems engineering and the long-term trajectory extends toward a systematic qualification approach for AI usage within SE.

\paragraph{Cognitive-Difficulty as a Structural Benchmark Dimension.}
Future versions of SysEngBench should incorporate mandatory cognitive-depth labeling (e.g., Bloom-level or an equivalent engineering reasoning taxonomy) and report performance conditioned on difficulty rather than aggregate accuracy alone. Difficulty-aware reporting would enable qualification decisions aligned to task complexity and help identify capability cliffs that remain obscured in overall averages.

\paragraph{Multi-modal Artifact Integration.}
Systems engineering artifacts are not purely textual, and future benchmarking should include diagram interpretation, text–model alignment, and cross-artifact consistency tasks reflective of the multi-modal nature of \ac{se}. Evaluating vision-language models and multi-modal pipelines on these representations would provide increased qualification evidence more directly aligned to model-based engineering workflows.

\paragraph{Human–AI Performance Comparison.}
Future research could systematically compare strengths, limitations, and failure modes of humans and LLMs across representative engineering tasks, establishing empirically grounded competency metrics for LLMs relative to that of human, systems engineering experts. Such studies, likely requiring Institutional Review Board oversight, would support mapping safe operating envelopes and defining regions where human oversight remains essential.

\paragraph{Structured Multi-Agent.}
There are many circumstances within \ac{se} that could leverage multi-agent deliberation mechanisms similar to that of domain subject matter experts adjudication. Systematically studying consensus formation, ensemble disagreement, and adversarial critique could provide additional information for systems engineers to make informed trade space decisions.

\paragraph{Cost-Aware AI Qualification.}
Building on the token-efficiency analysis presented in this dissertation, future research should formalize economic models that capture inference cost, robustness replication overhead, and scoring expenses. Such models would support trade studies that determine how much evaluation evidence is sufficient for a given risk posture and budget constraint.
